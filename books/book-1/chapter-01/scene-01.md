Two fingers in the dark. Anton on the far wall, his back to the indigo and his crowbar across both forearms, not because he needed the rest but because he was the kind of man who stood that way, weight already forward, like the wall behind him was a suggestion. The charge was already against the lock. I'd been crouched in this passage for forty seconds while he waited and the cooking-fume residue found the back of my throat. I held two fingers up and started counting.

He was twice as wide as I was and I am not a narrow woman.

I'd been assured he was reliable. I had been assured this by Detective Ford, who was currently waiting in a courtyard three levels up and who had outsourced the reliability question entirely to me.

The lock blew inward on count two. The crack rang off the crystal walls and came back twice — once from the corridor ahead and once from the tunnel behind us — and the indigo dust hit my gorget and my cheeks and got in my teeth and up my nose. Shit. Standard. I spat once and went in.

The corridor was dark, lit thirty feet ahead by a single sconce that was pushing the shadow back to a middling grey. The walls were the same substrate as every wall on the Skylands, the same indigo crystal that had been grown or carved or both out of the fount's outgrowth since before my grandmother was born. You stopped seeing it after a while. It became wallpaper. Tonight it was just walls between me and the cooking floor, and the cooking floor was where I needed to be.

I had the repeating revolver in my right hand and the sword in my left. Kendall's revolver — six rounds, crystal-cased shot, the grip fitted to my particular hand so precisely that for a long time I thought he'd cast it off a mold of my grip while I was sleeping, which given Kendall was not impossible. The gauntlet on my right wrist, also Kendall's, was dormant, strapped and warm. The Powerstone seated in my gorget was at full charge. I'd been told the guards inside were not armoured, so I'd look before I started spending from the stone.

Two guards at the first bend. They'd heard the breach and gotten as far as drawing. I came around the corner while they were still registering the situation and put a round through the first one's throat, and he was down before his partner understood what was happening. Anton came in behind me and the crowbar took the second one across the temple with the sound of something wet and dense hitting something hollow. The man dropped against the indigo wall and slid down it and was still.

I didn't stop. Consequential motions — Kendall's teaching, from a long time back when I was young enough that he'd been physically pulling me along with him in hallways not unlike this one, and the lesson had been about staying mobile rather than about anything violent, but the principle held. You move until you have a reason to stop.

The corridor dog-legged left through a passage narrow enough that Anton had to angle himself sideways to fit. I could smell the cooking floor now. The fumes came through the walls, or seemed to — a sharp chemical smell with something sweet underneath it, the sweetness wrong in a way that sat in the back of the throat and stayed there. Cook-fumes from a Stardust batch in progress. I knew the smell from the last two times Ford had sent me down here and from half a dozen contracts before that and I still couldn't get used to it, though I also couldn't have told you why, because the smell was simply a fact about certain rooms and not a moral claim.

A strip of yellow light showed at the bottom of a door at the far end of the passage. Female voices behind it, working voices, a woman complaining about yield and another telling her to keep the heat steady and a third offering an opinion nobody had asked for.

I held up a fist. Anton stopped.

I stood there and listened for thirty seconds. No guard-voices in the working talk. Either Ford's count was right and the guards were inside the room proper rather than posted at the door, or Ford's count was wrong and there were more of them somewhere I hadn't seen yet. Both were possible. Ford was a good detective and a mediocre intelligence source.

I had a shield-breaker on my belt, one of Kendall's recent gifts, which he'd handed over with the specific instruction to use it only on installations I was certain could afford the damage. It would go through a standard projector-warded door without complaint. But the door ahead looked like plain crystal with a standard mechanical lock — no shimmer at the edges that would mean a shield field. I tried the latch. Unlocked.

I pushed it open with my shoulder, held the weight so it didn't carry into the wall, and went through.

The cooking floor was a wide oval, maybe forty feet across. Six Powerstone sconces set in the walls, all of them dialled low, and two hanging chemical lanterns above the central tables — cook-fumes are flammable if you let magic get too close, and the cartel was not about to let their product and their workers and the whole operation go up in the same flash. The low Powerstones meant shadows angling sideways across the long tables, and the lanterns meant the work surface was lit and the corners were dark.

Eleven women at the tables. Ford had counted eight. Three weeks out of date, then, which was Ford's usual margin of error and not a disaster as long as the exit cleared before things got complicated.

The women wore thin wrap dresses with no seams to speak of and headbands that covered their hairlines. No pockets. House policy on the cooking floor — the cartel didn't trust anyone near the product, including the people they were paying twenty Coin a week to produce it. That was the shape of it: the legal Powerstones were running thirty Coin a week with the delivery fee, and these women couldn't afford them, so they were down here instead breathing residue and going home with their lungs full of sweet-smelling shit. Of course. The most they could do about that was keep the heat steady.

The two guards saw me immediately.

One of them had a muzzle-loader and got it to his shoulder faster than I'd have expected from a man in that lighting. Fuck — I put a round through his knee before I'd finished the thought and he screamed and dropped, which was less clean than I would have liked but cleaner than giving him a second to aim. The other guard was already moving for cover, a support pillar near the east wall, and I heard the hammer cock on his weapon as I went flat behind a table and the round discharged over my head and punched into the far wall with a sound like splitting crystal and came back as white powder.

The women went in several directions at once. Some flat, some running for the far door, one frozen at her table with both hands pressed to the tabletop as if that would help. I left them alone. Ford's contract was the books in the inner room and the merchandise — not the workers, not the bodies of the guards, and not any woman who had been paid twenty Coin a week to stand in chemical fumes all night.

"Drop it," I called. "I don't want your post."

He didn't drop it. They never drop it when you call from concealment, because they can't see you and they're stupid pricks who think the angle will come to them. He came around the pillar low, trying for it, and I was already flat with the revolver pointed at the gap between the pillar edge and the wall, and when his head came through I shot him in the temple. He sat down against the stone and stopped.

Anton had taken the kneecapped guard.

I didn't need to look at what that had involved. The sounds told me enough, and the duration told me more — the man was down and posed no further tactical concern from the moment I shot him, and the sounds coming from that part of the room lasted longer than a single incapacitating blow required. Anton stopped when he stopped. Not when the threat stopped.

I stood up from behind the table. The Powerstone had dropped almost a third of its charge from the two rounds — the crystal shot costs more from the stone than a standard load, and I'd been spending since the breach. I did a quick calculation: enough for the inner room, enough for whatever was in there, enough to get back out. Probably.

I reloaded. Six rounds, smooth and quick, three years of muscle memory in Kendall's custom grip. The Powerstone in my gorget ticked faintly as it redistributed the remaining charge through the armour's channels, the way it always did when it got low enough to matter.

Anton straightened up behind me. I heard his joints pop — shoulders, neck, the heavy vertebral crack that meant he'd rolled his whole back. His breathing had come down from where it had been, but it was the shallow-fast of something held in check rather than something finished. The crowbar was wet at the heavy end. He held it loosely.

"Clean?" he said.

"Close enough," I said. "Put the bar down."

He looked at the bar, then at me, then at the bar again.

"Put it down," I said. "Against the wall. We're not done."

He set it against the wall. Slowly enough that I noticed the slowness. The movement was careful in the way that something held tight is careful, not in the way that something at ease is careful.

I turned back to the arched passage at the north end of the room. The shielded inner room was through there. I could feel the projector-warded seal humming across the arch even at this distance, a low pressure through the Powerstone in my gorget, and the shield-breaker on my belt answered at the same frequency. Anton's crowbar leaned at the edge of my vision, still wet, and my hand found the shield-breaker's grip without being told to.
