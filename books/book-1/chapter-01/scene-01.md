Two fingers in the dark. Anton on the far wall, his back to the indigo and his crowbar across both forearms, weight already forward, like the wall behind him was a suggestion he'd stopped entertaining. Even standing still he was throwing off heat — I could see it in the cold passage air, the faint distortion above his shoulders, the way the skin along his neck had already gone the deep red-pink of an engine warming up. The seam-scars ran from his trapezius down into the collar of his sleeveless vest, the surgical lines where the graft fibres had been threaded through the muscle bed, and his breathing was already the shallow-fast of something pre-loaded. The graft was engaging. He hadn't run yet. The graft was anticipating.

He was twice as wide as I was and I am not a narrow woman.

The charge was already against the lock. I'd been crouched in this passage for forty seconds while he waited, and the cooking-fume residue found the back of my throat, and now I held two fingers up and started counting.

Ford had told me he was reliable. Ford was three levels up in a courtyard, where assurances about other people cost nothing.

The lock blew inward on count two. The crack rang off the crystal walls and came back twice — once from the corridor ahead and once from the tunnel behind us — and the indigo dust hit my gorget and my cheeks and got in my teeth and up my nose. Shit. Standard. I spat once and went in.

The corridor was dark, lit thirty feet ahead by a glow globe that was pushing the shadow back to a middling grey. The walls were the same substrate as every wall on the Skylands, the same indigo crystal that had been grown or carved or both out of the fount's outgrowth since before my grandmother was born. You stopped seeing it after a while. It became wallpaper. Tonight it was just walls between me and the cooking floor, and the cooking floor was where I needed to be.

I had the repeating revolver in my right hand and the sword in my left. Kendall's revolver — six casings, crystal shot, the grip fitted to my particular hand so precisely that for a long time I thought he'd cast it off a mold while I was sleeping, which given Kendall was not impossible. The gauntlet on my right wrist, also Kendall's, was dormant, strapped and warm. The Powerstone seated in my gorget was at full charge. I'd been told the guards inside were not armoured, so I'd look before I started spending from the stone.

Two guards at the first bend. They'd heard the breach and gotten as far as drawing. I came around the corner while they were still registering the situation and put a round through the first one's throat, and he was down before his partner understood what was happening. Anton came in behind me and the crowbar took the second one across the temple with the sound of something wet and dense hitting something hollow. The man dropped against the indigo wall and slid down it.

The crowbar swing had been past necessary. So would the next one. I kept moving.

I didn't stop. Consequential motions — Kendall's teaching, from a long time back when I was young enough that he'd been physically pulling me along with him in hallways not unlike this one, and the lesson had been about staying mobile rather than about anything violent, but the principle held. You move until you have a reason to stop.

The corridor dog-legged left through a passage narrow enough that Anton had to angle himself sideways to fit, and even then I heard the grunt of his shoulders catching the crystal walls on both sides. Behind me he was already breathing louder than I'd have liked. I could smell the cooking floor now. The fumes came through the walls, or seemed to — a sharp chemical smell with something sweet underneath it, the sweetness wrong in a way that sat in the back of the throat and stayed there. Cook-fumes from a Stardust batch in progress. I knew the smell from the last two times Ford had sent me down here and from half a dozen contracts before that. It was simply a fact about certain rooms.

No voices from behind the door at the end of the passage. A strip of yellow light showed at the bottom, and nothing else. Whatever the women inside had been saying to each other had stopped — the crack of the lock blowing would have carried through the crystal clear as a bell. I held up a fist. Anton stopped.

I stood there and listened for thirty seconds. The silence behind the door was working silence. If they were hiding, they'd have killed the lanterns. Ford was a good detective and a mediocre intelligence source.

I had a shield-breaker on my belt, one of Kendall's recent gifts, which he'd handed over with the specific instruction to use it only on installations I was certain could afford the damage. The door ahead looked like plain crystal with a standard mechanical lock — no shimmer at the edges that would mean a shield field. I tried the latch. Unlocked.

I pushed it open with my shoulder, held the weight so it didn't carry into the wall, and went through.

The cooking floor was a wide oval, maybe forty feet across. A half-dozen glow globes were set at intervals in the walls, all of them running low, and two hanging chemical lanterns above the central tables — cook-fumes are flammable if you let magic get too close, and the cartel was not about to let their product and their workers and the whole operation go up in the same flash. The low glow globes meant shadows angling sideways across the long tables, and the lanterns meant the work surface was lit and the corners were dark.

Eleven women at the tables. Ford had counted eight. Three weeks out of date, then, which was Ford's usual margin of error.

The women were near-naked, headbands covering their hairlines, nothing else worth speaking of. The cartel's policy on the cooking floor: undress at the door, dress again when you leave, nothing in which to conceal product on your person. Twenty Coin a week. Legal Powerstones were running thirty Coin a week with the delivery fee, and these women couldn't afford them, so they were down here instead breathing residue and going home with their lungs full of sweet-smelling shit, and if they stopped moving their hands for longer than it took to cough, someone hit them. Skylander rates. The wage was the leash.

Every one of them had heard the lock blow. Every one of them had their hands moving on the work surfaces. Their eyes were on the door.

The two guards saw me at the same moment.

One had a muzzle-loader and got it to his shoulder faster than I'd have expected from a man in that lighting. I put a round through his knee before I'd finished the thought and he screamed and dropped — less clean than I would have liked, but cleaner than giving him a second to aim. The other guard was already moving for cover, a support pillar near the east wall, and I heard his hammer cock as I went flat behind a table and the round discharged over my head and punched into the far wall with a sound like splitting crystal and came back as white powder.

"Drop it," I called. "I don't want your post."

He didn't drop it. They never drop it when you call from concealment, because they can't see you and they're stupid pricks who think the angle will come to them. He came around the pillar low, and I was already flat with the revolver pointed at the gap between the pillar edge and the wall, and when his head came through I shot him in the temple. He sat down against the stone and stopped.

Anton had the kneecapped guard.

The sounds from that corner went on longer than necessary and then turned into something else. I was checking the Powerstone reading on my gorget — the charge was almost untouched, the casings pulling regeneration from the ambient field the way they always did on the Skylands, the dense magic of the city handling it without a twitch. Off the city it would have been a different accounting. On the Skylands, I could have emptied the cylinder and barely noticed. I flexed my wrist over the cylinder out of reflex and found the spent chambers already regenerated.

The screaming started from the north end of the room. A woman's voice, then two, then the sound of something heavier.

Anton was in graft-flood. The room was his now. Whatever was in it with him belonged to whatever the graft was telling him about the room. Ford had hired him. I had work in the next room and was not paid to manage other people's chemistry.

I walked toward the arched passage at the north end of the room, the shield-breaker in my hand, thumb finding the projector-warded seal by memory.

The screaming continued. Behind me and to my left, through the glow-globe light, Anton's shoulder caught the angle of the nearest globe as he moved — the graft-flush deep across the skin, steam coming off him in the cooler air of the cooking floor. I did not look back. The hum of the shielded inner-room seal found its frequency in my bones, low and exact. I stepped under the arch.
