Two fingers in the dark. Anton on the far wall, his back to the indigo and his crowbar across both forearms, weight already forward, like the wall behind him was a suggestion he'd stopped entertaining. Even standing still he was throwing off heat — I could see it in the cold passage air, the faint distortion above his shoulders, the way the skin along his neck had already gone the deep red-pink of an engine warming up. The seam-scars ran from his trapezius down into the collar of his sleeveless vest, the surgical lines where another man's muscle had been threaded into his — cadaver strips for the legal grafts, live donors for the cheaper ones, neither variety advertised on the surgeon's slate. His breathing was already the shallow-fast of something pre-loaded. The graft was engaging. He hadn't run yet. The graft was anticipating.

He was twice as wide as I was and I am not a narrow woman.

The breach-spell was set against the lock — my work, an hour earlier, traced into the bolt-mechanism in a held-circuit closed off my fingertip. It would burn through when I closed the loop. I'd been crouched in this passage for forty seconds while Anton waited and the cooking-fume residue found the back of my throat, and now I held up two fingers, my count-gesture, and started bringing them down.

Ford had told me Anton was reliable. Ford was three levels up in a courtyard, where assurances about other people cost nothing.

The lock blew inward when my second finger touched my palm. The crack rang off the crystal walls and came back twice — once from the corridor ahead and once from the tunnel behind us — and the indigo dust hit my gorget and my cheeks and got in my teeth and up my nose. Shit. I spat once and went in.

The corridor was dark, lit thirty feet ahead by a glow globe that was pushing the shadow back to a middling grey. The walls were the same substrate as every wall on the Skylands, the same indigo crystal grown out of the fount's outgrowth since before my grandmother was born. The grain caught what light there was in long oblique striations, the way crystal does. It had stopped registering for me years ago. It was the only material I had ever stood on.

I had the repeating revolver in my right hand and the sword in my left. Kendall's revolver — six casings, crystal shot, the grip fitted to my particular hand so precisely that for a long time I thought he had cast it off a mold while I was sleeping, which given Kendall was not impossible. The gauntlet on my right wrist, also Kendall's, was dormant, strapped and warm. The Powerstone seated in my gorget was at about three-quarter charge — that was the day's mana in it, what I had bought off the home dispenser this morning, and there was no charger inside the manufactory I would trust to top me up for free. I had been told the guards inside were not armoured. I would look before I started spending what was left.

Two guards at the first bend. They had heard the breach and gotten as far as drawing. I came around the corner while they were still trying to find their lines of sight and put a round through the first one's throat, and he was down before his partner had registered the geometry. Anton came in behind me and the crowbar took the second one across the temple with the sound of something wet and dense hitting something hollow. The skull-plate caved in on itself; the man dropped against the indigo wall and slid down it leaving a wide red streak.

The crowbar had hit harder than the job needed. The next one would too. I kept moving.

Move until something stops you. Kendall's lesson, drilled in when I was small enough that he was physically dragging me through corridors not unlike this one to make me learn it — the lesson was about mobility, not violence, but the principle held. Until something gave you a reason to stop.

The corridor jogged left through a passage narrow enough that Anton had to angle himself sideways to fit, and even then I heard the grunt of his shoulders catching the crystal walls on both sides. Behind me he was already breathing louder than I would have liked. I could smell the cooking floor now. The fumes came through the walls, or seemed to — a sharp chemical smell with something sweet underneath it, the sweetness wrong in a way that sat in the back of the throat and stayed there. Cook-fumes from a Stardust batch in progress. I knew the smell from the last two times Ford had sent me down here and from half a dozen contracts before that. It was simply a fact about certain rooms.

No voices from behind the door at the end of the passage. A strip of yellow light showed at the bottom, and nothing else. Whatever the women inside had been saying to each other had stopped — the crack of the lock blowing would have carried through the crystal clear as a bell. I held up a fist. Anton stopped.

I stood there and listened for thirty seconds. The silence behind the door was working silence. If they were hiding they would have killed the lights. Ford was a good detective and a mediocre intelligence source.

The door ahead looked like plain crystal with a standard mechanical lock. I tried the latch. Unlocked.

I pushed it open with my shoulder, held the weight so it did not carry into the wall, and went through.

The cooking floor was a wide oval, maybe forty feet across. Glow globes were set at intervals in the walls and overhead, a few brighter ones over the long central tables where the cooks needed to see what their hands were doing, the rest dimmed almost to nothing. Glow globes ran on mana, and mana cost money, and even the cartel rationed: light the work, leave the corners dark.

Eleven women at the tables. Ford had counted eight. Ford undercounted; he usually did.

The women were near-naked, headbands covering their hairlines, nothing else worth speaking of. The cartel's policy on the cooking floor: undress at the door, dress again when you leave, nothing in which to conceal product on your person. There was no other work to be had for them, not down here, not for a woman who did not have a Mage's mark on her shoulder. If they stopped moving their hands for longer than it took to cough, someone hit them.

Every one of them had heard the lock blow. Every one of them had her hands moving on the work surfaces. Their eyes were on the door.

The two guards saw me at the same moment.

One had a muzzle-loader and got it to his shoulder faster than I would have expected from a man in that lighting. I put a round through his knee before I had finished the thought and the kneecap came out the back of his leg in three pieces; he screamed and dropped. The other guard was already moving for cover, a support pillar near the east wall, and I heard his hammer cock as I went flat behind a table and the round discharged over my head and punched into the far wall with a sound like splitting crystal and came back as white powder.

"Drop it," I called. "You can walk."

He did not drop it. They never do when you call from concealment, because they cannot see you and they are stupid pricks who think the angle will come to them. He came around the pillar low, and I was already flat with the revolver pointed at the gap between the pillar edge and the wall, and when his head came through I shot him in the temple. He sat down against the stone and stopped.

Anton had the kneecapped guard. Graft-craze had Anton by then — graft-flood doesn't stop when the threat stops, it stops when it stops, and Anton was no longer driving.

The crowbar came down on the man's chest, the ribcage giving way the way a crystal panel does under a hammer. Then on the face: the lower jaw folded sideways and tore free of its hinge, the tongue coming with it, and the second strike took the side of the skull off above the ear and stopped the screaming.

The graft turned him toward the women.

Graft-flood doesn't filter by threat — anything in motion in the room is the same problem the guards had been. The closest woman was too slow getting up from the table. His free hand caught the back of her head and put her face into the work surface hard enough that the crystal slab cracked across the centre. She went limp. He pulled her up by the hair and threw her across the floor without looking at where she landed; she came to rest beside the dead pillar guard, headband off, mouth open, not moving.

The next one ran. She made it three steps toward the door I had come through. The graft was on her before five — graft-craze sees a runner and the graft does the math on closing the distance without bothering Anton about it. He took her by the shoulder and the joint came apart with a noise like wet leather tearing, the arm coming away in his hand still in its socket-cuff of muscle. The graft was already moving past her; the rest of her ran for two more steps before her body caught up to what had happened to it and went down. He came back around and hit her in the back of the head with the crowbar to be sure.

The third — older, slower, did not try to run. The graft did not care; graft-flood eats the still ones too. Crowbar across the throat. Crowbar across the temple. She folded to the floor in two separate pieces of motion. She was bleeding from the throat before her head touched the crystal.

The screaming had spread from the north end of the room. The graft kept Anton going.

I checked the Powerstone reading on my gorget. The breach-spell had taken real charge from it — the spell was the cost, not the firing — and there were four spent chambers in the cylinder I had not bothered to cycle. The casings only drew when I activated them, and I was not activating until the arch.

Anton was deep in graft-flood and the room was his until the graft burned out. Ford had hired him. I had work in the next room. Babysitting a graft-craze was not in the contract.

I walked toward the arched passage at the north end of the room, the shield-breaker in my hand, thumb finding the projector-warded seal by memory.

The screaming continued. Behind me and to my left, through the glow-globe light, I caught Anton in motion — wet to the elbows, the crowbar slick the full length of it, blood across his chest in long arterial sprays, more across his jaw and his ear where someone had opened at close range. The graft-flush still ran underneath all of it, steam coming off the skin in the cooler air of the cooking floor, the dead-meat strips at his collarbones twitching with the graft-craze. The graft does not look down at its own arms. I did not look back either. The hum of the shielded inner-room seal found its frequency in my bones, low and exact. I stepped under the arch.
