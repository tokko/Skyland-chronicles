I was two blocks from the platform when a drunkard in a mound of mouldy spore-cloth called something at my back. I didn't catch the words. I kept walking.

The station was a ledge of indigo crystal with a low barrier and a single bench and a glow globe at the far end throwing the wrong shade for the hour, lavender where it should have been dim violet, a dying globe no one had thought to replace. I leaned against the pillar at the platform's edge and waited.

The mana stream ran along the underside of the rail track, a thick pulsing cord of violet-white power as wide as my thigh. I could feel it on magesense from where I was standing: dense, steady, hot with stored energy. The Powerstone in my choker was low. Dissolving the gorget and corset presets had clawed back a little, but the breach-spell had eaten most of what it held and the night had not given it time to recover. The stream was right there.

A Guard stood at the far end of the platform. Full chain-and-plate, crystal lenses in the visor, breech-loader on his shoulder with the muzzle down. He was watching me the way I had been watching the stream. His eyes didn't move to my face. They stayed at my throat, reading the Powerstone in the choker the same way I'd read the stream.

The Powerstone was Bound to my Soul. Useless to him. But mana-theft from the public infrastructure was the one crime the Guard actually enforced, because the Guard's own budget came off the same ley-line draw. Everything else in the Undercity could be bought, sold, or ignored. Not the power.

I took my hand off my magesense and looked at the dark wall across the track.

The tram arrived. I boarded without looking at him.

The Skyway went underground first, the way it always did. Tunnel walls close on either side, glow globes strobing past the windows in their regular count. Then the tunnel opened and the tram launched out from the Skylands' underside into open air, and the bottom dropped out of the world.

The Barrier was off to the left: a sphere of brilliant blue that flamed around the city at every point, the edge of everything I had ever known. The light off it turned the tram's interior blue for a few seconds before the arc of the track curved us away. Below the city, where the sphere opened at its base, the Great Geyser spewed its column of mana from the ocean floor and the collector ribs on the Skylands' underside drank from it. The gap was the only place the Barrier was permeable. Everything else: sealed, and had been since before anyone breathing could say when.

The ocean was down there through the gap, too far to make out individual swells, the water always in churn around the Geyser's base.

A satellite Skylands drifted past the window, a few hundred yards to starboard, a crystal shelf no bigger than two or three good apartment blocks, a cluster of buildings on top, the tether of its ley-line umbilical running back to the main monolith like a rope. Lit up. Clean lines. No drunkards in spore-cloth outside those gates.

I had a recurring thought about moving Mom there. It surfaced every time the tram went around this side, regular as the glow globes strobing in the tunnel. The thought never got any more realistic. Merc money was good money if you didn't blow it on Stardust and whores and booze the way most of the trade did, and I did not blow it on those things, and the satellite address was still out of reach. The thought was just that — a thought. Something to look at through the window for the thirty seconds the satellite was in view.

Then it was past, and the tram followed the arc of the stream around the Skylands' belly and came up the other side.

My stop was the fourth. I got off and walked home with my hand on the backup revolver inside my coat. The neighbourhood required it. Nothing tried me. Ten minutes, quiet, and I was at the door.

One mechanical lock. I keyed it and went in and stood in the dark a moment before I found the home dispenser and switched it on. The household containment field hummed up around me, low ambient, warm, the specific frequency of home that nowhere else quite matched. I set the spent casings in the recharge cradle beside the dispenser. Full by morning. The cost of tonight would show on the meter by end of week.

I unstrapped the gauntlet and set it on the table, then the gunbelt. Primary revolver on the hook, backup beside it. The longer sword. The belt-pouch of loose casings. The shorter blade on the shelf above the bed. Under the coat I was down to the choker.

The coat I left materialised while I dug Raynard's bills out of the pocket and set them with Ford's on the table. Then I dissolved the coat too, the indigo vapour drawing back into the Powerstone. I had let the bra and thong go back into the choker before I left the manufactory and never re-formed them. Just the choker now.

Someone like me should not own a Powerstone like the one in that choker. Prohibitively expensive did not cover it — the materials were military-commission grade, the enchantment work was bespoke, and the Soul-Binding alone took a Mage of Kendall's calibre a month to complete. If the Binding hadn't been there, someone would have cut my throat for it years ago. The Binding meant the stone was worthless to anyone else. It meant it was mine, entirely, until I stopped existing. My best friend was a genius, and he had built it for my twenty-fifth birthday, and I would never be able to pay him back for it in any currency that mattered, and he knew that, and he had done it anyway.

Water was expensive. I ran the tub.

I sat on the edge and watched the steam rise off the incoming water. The tap ran hot on the night cycle; the building's heat-fount kept running until just before the first bell and the water would not be this hot by morning. I let the tub fill most of the way, turned off the tap, and sank in up to my chin.

The muscles let go one at a time. The ribs first, because they had been holding since the cooking-rig hall, that long specific clench of taking a hit you absorb and don't drop for. The shoulders, because the gauntlet shield had been pulling them forward all night. The back, because the shorter blade's sheath had been riding the same vertebra for hours. The inside of my thighs last, because Raynard, for reasons I preferred not to name more precisely than that.

The battle-rush was gone. The thing that came after it was not rest. It was the flat grey nothing that settled when the adrenaline burned off and there was nothing left to fill in the space it had occupied. The night had been a good night by the numbers, clean breach, the job done, Ford's second payment in my pocket, most of my casings still in service. A good night. The money on that table was good money.

The encounter with Raynard had eaten about half of what a good night was supposed to feel like.

I reached down and traced myself with my fingertips and drew my hand back with a wince. Swollen. Raw. That bastard.

I submerged until the water closed over my face and held it there, held until I stopped counting and the pressure in my lungs became a colour behind my eyes, held a little longer than that, then pushed back up. I came up gasping. Pushed the wet hair off my face. Breathed.

The bruise was already colouring at my throat where his hand had gripped. I could feel the edge of it without touching it, that specific warmth of a fresh bruise doing its work in the dark. The bills were on the table. The spent casings were in the cradle. The shorter blade was on the shelf.

I soaked until the water went cold, which did not take long.

Then I got out, towelled off, draped the towel over the tub rail, and went to bed. The drain made a slow sound behind the curtain as it worked through the cooled water. I lay in the dark and listened to it empty, and then it finished, and the apartment was quiet.
