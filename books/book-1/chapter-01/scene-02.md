The shield dropped with the sound of a projector dying. A high flat tone that fell through three registers and cut off. The arch frame went dark where the emitter had been, its housing scorched the colour of old scar tissue. I let the spent shield-breaker fall against the wall and stepped through.

The inner sanctum was better lit than the cooking floor. Whoever ran this site had spent real mana on it: four glow globes burning at full strength above a long central rig, the benches clean enough to eat off, the canisters on the shelves labelled in etched crystal rather than scratched brass. The Stardust here was the refined grade, the expensive kind, the kind Ford had said went to the Surface buyers. Quality operation. I spent about half a second noticing this before I registered the bayonets.

Four men. Guard-issue smoothbores, fixed bayonets, the kind of kit that cost more than a month of cooking-floor wages to maintain. They had not bought it themselves. Off-duty Guard, the same as always, the same as every cartel site with cash to spend on something more reliable than hungry men with muzzle-loaders. They had not wasted the time I had given them cracking the projector: they had moved the women into a line in front of them before I came through.

Eight women at the inner rigs. Near-naked, headbands, the same as outside. They had not run. Running would have meant standing up from the bench, and then someone would have hit them. The three in the front row now had a bayonet at the level of each of their spines and Guard hands at each of their elbows, and the fourth guardsman stood behind the shelter of the corner where the ley-line conduit ran up the wall.

I took that in and stepped left along the wall, keeping low, drawing the bayonet team's eyes off the doorway and buying myself a full look at the room.

The conduit was the thing I clocked second. It came up through the floor at the far wall, ran through a brass fitting at knee-height that nobody had bothered to conceal, and then split at the ceiling into four branches feeding the rigs and the glow globes. The fitting was three times the width a standard building-line ran, fattened up with added casings until the junction wore extra metal like a man wearing two coats. Jury-built. Even from across the room I could feel the draw of it on my magesense. The conduit was pulling from the Undercity's main ley-line channel, not from any metered distribution point. Not their power to spend. Nobody down here was paying for it.

The women in front of the bayonet team were not the same problem as the junction in the wall, but the cartel's calculus on both was the same. Use what you can get your hands on. Put a cost on anyone who tries to take it back.

I pressed my thumb across the cylinder face. Four strokes, one per spent chamber, the standard catch. The enchantments in the casings pulled from the gorget-stone and I felt the Powerstone's charge drop as four new slugs grew in the chambers. The reading was maybe half now, possibly less. I noted it and moved on.

The lead guardsman was the one not holding a woman. He had his smoothbore up and was tracking me along the wall, his head low behind the barrel. Professional. The problem was geometry: he had not come far enough forward from the corner to give me a clear angle on his shoulder, and the women in the front were blocking his partners' bodies below the collarbone.

"Put them down," he said. He meant the revolver and the sword. His voice was the flat command register of the Guard, the register Micah had drilled into me when I served. "On the floor."

"Your mates are going to shoot their friends in the back if they miss me," I said. "You know that. Move the women."

"On the floor. Now."

The three women in front row were not moving. The nearest one had her eyes forward and her hands still flat on the bench she had just been pulled from, the force of habit of thirty-hour shifts stronger than the instinct to run. She was the oldest one in the room. The skin on her forearms was loosened by years of chemical contact, the lines at the outer corners of her eyes set deep. She was looking at a specific point on the far wall and her lips were moving without making sound.

I shot the lead guardsman through the ankle.

He went down behind the corner and his shot went into the ceiling when he lost the grip. The women at the benches went flat with the sound of bodies hitting crystal in rapid sequence, the older woman last, rolling off her bench and pulling her knees to her chest on the floor. The two guardsmen holding partners in the front dropped their women. Not out of mercy; they went for their triggers when their officer went down, and you need both hands for a smoothbore. The partners scattered.

Four shots. The leftmost guardsman first, through the upper chest, the round punching through the gorget-gap above his armour plate and dropping him clean. The second in the sternum, the shot going wide of the heart but catching enough of it that he sat down hard and did not stand back up. The fourth guardsman had gotten behind the ley-line conduit and I put a round into the brass fitting at knee-height just below his hands. The fitting took the round badly, a chunk of the outer casing spinning loose across the floor, and the sound of it made him step left into the gap, which was what I had wanted. I shot him through the knee, the same geometry as outside. He folded.

The lead guardsman was dragging himself along the floor toward his weapon, his ankle leaving a wide red smear on the crystal. I stepped on his wrist. He made a sound. I put the revolver barrel against the back of his head at a downward angle and he went still.

"Done?" I said.

"Done," he said.

I took his weapon and put it in the corner. The two wounded guardsmen, ankle and knee, were not going anywhere. I left them making sounds about it and checked the room. Two of the women had run for a back passage I had not spotted. The others were still flat on the floor or crouched against the rigs.

The older woman had not moved from her knees-to-chest position on the floor.

"You can go," I said. "The passage you came in from. The front of the building."

She did not look at me. Her lips were still moving.

I left it.

The stairs to the upper level were in the back corner, the same corner the fourth guardsman had used for cover. Crystal steps worn smooth in the centre by years of foot traffic, the glow globe above the first landing broken and replaced by a jury-mounted brass bracket with a small power cell taped to it. A cheaper fix than requisitioning a proper globe. The kind of maintenance choice that told you the upper level was not where the real money was kept. I took the stairs two at a time, listening for movement above.

Nothing. The upper level was a storage corridor, not a working floor: crates of sealed canisters stacked against the walls, a narrow passage between them, glow globes burning low. At the far end, light from outside.

From behind me, below, I heard one of the wounded guardsmen stop making sounds.

Then the other one.

I did not go back to check. The graft was not in this room and the graft did not differentiate between guard and captive. I had known this before I came through the arch, the same as I had known the bayonet team would use the women as cover. Ford's intelligence was mediocre but it was usually right about the cartel's standard procedures.

The window at the end of the corridor was not a proper window, the crystal wall had been cut out and left open, the edges rough where the removing tool had gone slightly wide. The night air off the Undercity came through it, carrying the smell of the Bowels three levels below — fungus and the particulate of the Geyser channels, the low chemical note that had been part of the air since before I was born. I crossed to it and looked out.

The courtyard was maybe thirty feet below. Ford had a dozen men down there in Constabulary grey. Not the official kind. The kind that went on your record as a training exercise if anyone asked. The manufactory's surviving workers were lined up along the far wall, their backs to it. I counted seven. Two of them were holding each other by the wrist.

Ford was in the courtyard below me, his coat buttoned against the cold, his face tilted up toward the upper floor. Checking exits, probably. The habit of a methodical man. He had not seen me yet.

The two women holding wrists were separated by one of the grey-coat men. He was not rough about it. That was the detail I remembered afterward: that he was not rough about it.

One of Ford's men put a revolver to the back of the first worker's head and pulled the trigger.

I watched the Skyway tram pass on the level above, the carriages lit from inside and moving south toward the Geyser station. One of the middle-distance pressure plumes was up — the Geyser's overflow venting as it did twice a night, the white column of magic fluid catching the Barrier's filtered light on its way to the dome and dispersing. The plume had been doing that since before my grandmother's time.

Below, the second shot.

My hand was in my coat pocket. The empty chambers in the cylinder were regenerated; the Powerstone was at half, maybe below. I noted both and did nothing else.

The third shot.
