The Skyway station was three blocks east and I made it without anyone speaking to me or looking twice at what I was wearing. The Undercity at this hour was not the kind that looked twice.

The station was a crystal platform with a low rail and a single bench and a glow globe at the far end throwing the wrong colour for the time of night. The tram was not in yet. I leaned against the pillar at the platform's edge and waited.

The thick pulsing stream of mana that powered the tram ran along the underside of the rail track, a violet-white cord of raw power as wide as my arm. I could feel it on my magesense from the pillar: dense, steady, always running, the city's blood in its veins. The Powerstone in my choker was at better than half charge after the combat dissolution but it could always be more. The stream was right there. I could reach out and pull from it. I had done it before, years ago, when I had not known better.

A Guard stood at the far end of the platform, watching me through the visor slit in his chain-and-plate. His breech-loader was slung but his eyes were on my choker. He had read the Powerstone the same way I had read the stream and he was waiting to see if I would be stupid enough to try it in front of him. Mana-theft from the public infrastructure was the kind of crime the Guard actually cared about, because the Guard was paid by the same mana budget the stream came from. Everything else in this city could be bought, sold, ignored, or excused. Not the public power.

The tram arrived. I boarded without looking at the Guard.

The Skyway went underground first, the tunnel walls close on both sides, the glow globes flickering past the windows in a regular rhythm. Then the tunnel opened and the tram launched out from the underside of the Skylands into the open air and my stomach dropped the way it always did.

The Barrier was a hundred yards to the left, a sphere of brilliant blue that flamed around the city on all sides, enclosing everything I had ever known. The light off it filled the tram's windows on the port side and turned the interior blue for a moment before the tram curved away and the Barrier was behind us. Below the city, where the sphere opened, the Great Geyser of magic spewed up from the ocean floor and the collectors on the Skylands' underside drank from it. The opening was the only gap in the Barrier; the rest was sealed.

The ocean was down there, visible through the gap. Too far to see individual waves but I knew they were there, the water always in turmoil around the base of the Geyser. One of the satellite pressure plumes was visible from this angle, a white column of mana-fluid climbing from the ocean surface toward the collectors, catching the Barrier's light on its way up. The Geyser had been doing that since before anyone alive could remember. It was the reason we were up here at all.

A satellite Skylands floated past the window, a few hundred yards off. A handful of crystal buildings on a platform no bigger than a city block. There were a dozen of these around the periphery, each one tethered to the main mass by a ley-line umbilical that drew from the same Geyser. The rich lived on them. The views were better and the air was cleaner and the neighbours were fewer. I had a recurring fantasy of moving Mom to one of them. It was not a realistic fantasy. Merc money did not buy a satellite address. Not unless the merc was luckier than me, or more willing to do the kind of work I had so far managed to avoid.

The tram followed the arc of the mana stream down below the Skylands' belly and came up the other side. My stop was the fourth. I got off and walked the few minutes home without trouble, though my hand was on the backup revolver inside my coat the entire way. The neighbourhood was the kind where you kept your hand on something.

My apartment was a single room, a door with a mechanical lock and a small bathroom through a curtain at the back. I locked the door behind me and stood in the dark for a moment. The home dispenser on the wall was off. It was the only thing that fed mana into my apartment's containment field from the public ley lines, and I had shut it down before I left to cut the metered draw while I was out. I switched it back on. The field hummed to life around me, faint and warm, the familiar low-grade ambient that meant home.

I set the spent casings in the recharge cradle beside the dispenser. They would be full by morning. The cost of tonight's casings would show up on the household meter by the end of the week.

I touched the gem on the gauntlet and dissolved the coat. The indigo vapour drew inward to the choker and the physical items that had been in the coat's pockets dropped into my hand: the spent casings in their little cluster, Raynard's bills, Ford's bills. I set them on the table by the door. Then I unstrapped the gauntlet and put it beside them. Unbuckled the gunbelt. The revolvers went on the hook by the door, the swords beside them, the belt-pouch of loose casings on the table. The shorter blade went on the shelf above the bed.

I was standing in my apartment in the dark in nothing but the choker.

The bathroom through the curtain had a tub and a tap and nothing else. Water was expensive. Mana heated the water and mana cost money and I was tired enough that the cost was worth it. I turned the tap and let the tub fill while I sat on the edge and watched the steam rise. The water came hot because the building's communal boiler was still running on the night cycle. It would not be hot by morning.

I sank into the water up to my chin and felt the muscles let go one at a time. The ribs first because they had been holding since the cooking-rig hall. The shoulders because the gauntlet shield had been pulling them forward all night. The back because the shorter blade's sheath had been riding the same vertebra for hours. The inside of my thighs because Raynard.

The battle-rush was gone. What came after it was not rest. It was the flat grey quiet that settled in after the adrenaline burned off, and inside the quiet was the inventory of things I had done tonight that I would not think about tomorrow. The women on the cooking floor whose hands had not stopped moving. The bookkeeper on her knees. The girl with the bruise who had known it was coming. The sounds Anton had made in the rooms behind me. The sound Raynard had made against the back of my neck.

I reached down and traced myself with my fingertips. I withdrew with a wince. The flesh was swollen and raw from his handling. That bastard.

I slid down until the water covered my face and held my breath until I saw stars and my lungs screamed for air. I came up gasping and brushed the wet hair plastered across my face away. The water was starting to cool.

The bills were on the table by the door. The bruise on my throat where his hand had gripped was already starting to colour. There was a taste in my mouth I could not quite place. It was not the blood from the cheek-bite. It was something older than tonight.

I got out of the tub and towelled myself off. The water drained slowly. I left the tub to empty itself and went to bed.

The choker stayed at my throat. It always did.
