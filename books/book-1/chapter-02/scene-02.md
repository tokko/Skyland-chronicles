Tully's was four levels down from my apartment, in the part of the Undercity where the glow-globes were set far enough apart that there was real dark between them. The bar was a long slab of crystal grown straight up out of the floor, polished by elbows into a dull shine along its front edge. The stools were the same crystal, the same indigo, capped with discs of spore-felt so your ass did not go numb on the stone. Tully kept the place dim to save on globe-mana and dark enough that nobody looked too hard at anybody. It was the kind of bar where a merc could sit with her back to a wall and her drink in front of her and not be bothered. I liked it.

Greg was already there when I came in, two stools from the end, with a beer and an empty shot glass in front of him.

He lifted a hand when he saw me. Blue eyes, which were rare down here, and a face that had stayed handsome through a profession that ruined most faces. The curved short sword rode at his hip in a worn sheath. He could shoot, more or less, but the sword was what had made his name. I had watched him go into a knot of four men with it and come out the other side unhurt, the four of them in pieces behind him, the whole thing over before most mercs would have finished drawing. The shield projector on his gauntlet got him close enough to use it. Up close, with that blade, Greg was one of the most dangerous people I knew.

He was also, faced with a woman who knew what she was doing, hopelessly shy. I had learned years ago that I could make him lose his place in a sentence by leaning on the bar a certain way. I did it now, settling onto the stool beside him with my elbows on the crystal and my shoulders forward, and watched his eyes drop to my cleavage and snap back up like he had been caught stealing.

"Kyra," he said, recovering. "You look like you slept badly."

"My dispenser's dry again. Third time this month."

"The whole block?"

"The whole block." I waved at Tully for a beer. "Robin Hoods, if you believe it. Somebody's bleeding the lower mains."

Greg turned his shot glass in a slow quarter-circle on the crystal. "I heard a man two wards over found his neighbour drained dry in his bed. No mark on him. Just empty. Could be a story."

He said it the way he said everything, plain and unhurried, with no relish in it. For a merc he had appalling manners, by which I mean he had good ones. He thanked Tully when the beer came. He did not curse unless something deserved it.

Tully set the beer down and I put a coin on the counter. "What's the job?"

"Escort duty." He drank. "You reckon there'll be any action?"

"If there wasn't, they wouldn't have paid for two of us."

He grunted, half pleased and half resigned, the grunt of a man who would rather have a quiet day but would not turn down the chance to use the sword. "Who hired us?"

"No idea. Stardust, probably." I drank. The beer was the usual: fungal mash, fermented thin, more bitter than it had any right to be. "Which means I spent last night helping Ford burn a cook site and this morning I'm guarding the next batch. Don't say it."

"I wasn't going to say anything."

"You were thinking it."

"I think a lot of things." He smiled into his beer. "Money's money. You taught me that."

I had. It was the first thing I had told him when he was new and squeamish, six or seven years ago, before he had learned that the people you protect and the people you kill are sorted by who is paying and not by anything else. He had learned it. He had also, somehow, stayed kind, which was a trick I had never managed and had stopped trying to.

"How's the girl?" I asked.

His whole face changed. That was the thing about Greg. He could talk about a drained corpse without a flicker, but you said one word about his daughter and the merc went out of him and something open and stupid and soft came up in its place.

"Growing too fast," he said. "She's eleven now. Reads everything she can get. Tully's wife is teaching her letters proper, not just the trade slate." He turned the shot glass again. "She asked about you last week. Wanted to know if the lady with the purple hair was really the one I keep telling her about. I said she was. She wanted proof. I told her I'd ask if you'd come round and let her see for herself." He glanced sideways, almost shy about it on the girl's behalf. "She admires you, you know. Marissa. Thinks you're the bravest woman on the Skylands."

"She'll grow out of it."

"Maybe." He did not argue. He never pushed. "Come by sometime. She'd like to see you. You don't have to bring anything. She just likes you."

I said I would. I meant it the way I meant most things like that, which was that I would if the days lined up and I would feel bad about it if they didn't and they usually didn't. Greg knew that too. He let it sit.

Somewhere down the bar two dock-hands were arguing about the rich youths who had built the flying machine, the ones who had set out a few years back to breach the Barrier from the inside and prove there was something Outside worth reaching. The argument was the same argument it always was. One of them said they had nearly made it. The other said they had crashed into the Barrier and dropped into the Geyser in a long burning streak that half the Skylands had watched, and that there was nothing Outside, there had never been anything Outside, the Barrier was the world and the world was the Barrier and anyone who thought different could go join them in the water. I had watched the machine fall, years ago. I agreed with the second dock-hand. Fucking idiots, the lot of them.

"You must be Kyra and Greg."

I turned on my stool. The man standing behind us was nervous in the particular way of someone in the wrong neighbourhood. His eyes would not hold still. He shifted his weight foot to foot. His clothes were worth more than the bar took in a week, cut and dyed in a fashion I had only seen on the upper levels, and I gave him an hour before someone relieved him of them and possibly of his teeth.

"Yeah," I said. "What of it?"

His eyes settled on Greg, and he drew himself up and squared his shoulders, and despite being a head shorter than Greg he contrived to look down his nose at him. "I hired you."

Greg set his beer down. "Very well. And your name is?"

"It is not important. You may call me Ghost."

I snorted into my drink before I could stop myself. He shot me a wounded glare, like I was supposed to have heard the name before, like it meant something, like there were children in the upper districts who whispered it in the dark. I put as much mockery as I could fit into the next word.

"Alright. *Ghost.* What's the deal?"

"Security." He said it primly. "I have a business transaction scheduled and I require you to keep the peace."

I looked at Greg. Greg looked at me. No respectable transaction needed two armed mercs, and no respectable transaction happened in the Undercity, and this man had no more idea what he had walked into than the beer in my hand. We were not going to get anything useful out of him. We would have to go in blind and sort it out as it came.

I drained the beer and stood. Greg held up one finger, lifted his own mug, finished it without hurry, and set it down. Then he stood too.

"Lead the way," he said to Ghost, with a small courteous gesture, as though the man had asked him to dance.

Ghost spun on his heel and made for the door. I dropped another coin on the counter for the round, and Greg reached for his wallet, and I put my hand over his.

"This one's on me."

"You're too good to me." He fell into step beside me as we crossed the floor after our employer. "You know you're my favourite."

"You say that to all the girls."

"Only the ones I mean it with." He held the door. "Which is you."

He was lying through his teeth, and he knew I knew, and it warmed me anyway. It was not often anyone said a kind thing to me and meant for me to enjoy it. I let myself enjoy it.

We went out into the dark after the small ridiculous man, and I was easy in my body and easy in my head, and the last clean thing I did that night was hold Greg's beer for him while he checked the strap on his sword. Everything after that I would have given a great deal to undo.
