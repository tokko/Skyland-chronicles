The dispenser in the corner was dark.

I noticed it before I was fully awake. The hum it made when it was running, the low steady draw of the public ley line feeding mana into the apartment's containment field, had been there when I had gone to sleep and was not there now. The field was cold. I could feel the absence of it the way you feel the absence of a blanket: nothing touching you that should be touching you.

It was not a shutoff. My bill was paid.

I got up and crossed the room in the filtered grey-violet of the Barrier's dawn. The choker was warm at my throat; the Powerstone had its own charge independent of the apartment's field. The bills were still on the table by the door where I had left them. The gauntlet, the gunbelt, the revolvers on the hook. The shorter blade on the shelf above the bed. Everything where I had put it.

The dispenser's status-crystal was dead grey. I touched it. Nothing. The public ley line that fed this block was dry.

This was the third time this month.

I sat down on the rug in the middle of the room with the revolvers laid out in front of me and started reloading. The recharge cradle beside the dispenser had been running when I put the spent casings in last night; some of them had got enough charge before the outage hit. I checked each one by rolling it between my fingers. The charged ones had a faint warmth and a slight hum you could feel more than hear. Most were usable. A few were cold and dead and would need the dispenser back before they would take a slug again.

My hands were shaking very slightly as I loaded. Not from cold.

I did not name the reason. I broke the primary's cylinder open, seated the casings one at a time, and closed it. Then the backup. The motions were the same motions I had been making since Kendall had put the first revolver in my hand at sixteen. The shaking did not affect the loading. It never did.

The rumours about the Robin Hoods had been running through the lower districts for weeks. Someone was draining mana off the public mainlines that fed the lower wards. Not the upper wards; the upper wards had Guard-protected distribution centres with enchanted seals that would fry a thief's hands at the tap. Down here the lines were bare crystal conduit run through the crawlspaces between levels, and a man with a basic Construction Mage's training and a jury-rigged tap could bleed a whole block dry in a night.

The drained mana went somewhere. The sensible rumour said the cartels, who needed ambient for the curing and would pay over market for a supply that never touched a meter. The popular rumour said the Robin Hoods gave it away, ran it down to the poorest wards where the rates had climbed past what a factory wage could cover. I believed the sensible one. Altruism was a rumour that had never once survived contact with the actual Undercity.

The rates climbed because the Geyser fed the same mana it always had into a city that kept making more mouths. The Sky-Council raised the meter and let the market decide who got to stay warm. The market decided. The rich kept their fields lit. The rest of us learned to wake in the cold and not complain about it, because the dark crystal in the corner was cheaper to live beside than to fight.

I got up off the rug and went to the kitchen shelf. The shelf was a bracket on the wall with a single fungal-fibre basket. Inside: a half-round of pressed fungus and a small jar of spore-paste. I cut two slices of the pressed fungus, spread the spore-paste across both, and folded them together. The taste was the same taste it had been every morning for as long as I could remember: the flat, slightly sour base of the fungus and the sharper tang of the spore-paste, which was what passed for flavour in the lower wards.

I ate standing at the window, looking down at the street. The Barrier's light came through filtered and pale the way it did at this hour, not sunlight, never sunlight, only the grey-violet wash the dome made of whatever the sky threw at it. A woman crossed the far side with a child on her hip. The child had red hair.

I looked away.

It was the breakfast my father used to make me before his factory shift. He cut the slices thicker than I cut them now and ran the paste right to the edges, and he folded it and set it on my plate without a word, because the fourth bell came early and there was never time. He drank his water standing at the sink. On his way out he would put his hand on the back of my head, once, and then the door. The same sandwich. The same paste. The same flat sour taste, a childhood and a dead man later.

After the stroke took his legs and the Sky-Council declared him unfit for work, he had spent two weeks at the kitchen table reading the rates and the meat-market notices and saying nothing to Mom or to me. The end of the second week he had gone to the registry himself, on his crutches, and put his name in the book.

Clean meat was rare, and rare meant dear. Stardust ran through every tier of the city: cooked in the underworld, bought raw and caustic by the surface workers who could not afford better, and refined to something pure and almost traceless for the parties in the upper districts and the Spires. The richest took the cleanest of the drug and the cleanest of everything else. Most of us carried the residue in our tissue by our thirties. The ones who did not carried something worse, the diseases the lower wards traded hand to hand and bed to bed: the wasting cough, the sweating sickness, the rot that came up grey through the sheets if you lay down with the wrong people often enough. A body that had taken none of it was a delicacy. The butchery records called it untainted, and the word went on the price like a maker's mark. My father had never touched Stardust in his life. He had not been sick a day before the stroke took his legs out from under him. He was, by the measure the city used, a very good cut.

He told us at dinner the night before. He said he would not be a drain on the household. He said the payment would keep Mom in the apartment for two years if she was careful. He said he loved us.

That night I heard them through the wall. They thought I was asleep. Mom's voice was low and furious and breaking, the way a voice breaks when the person speaking has been crying for an hour without telling anyone, and his was low and even. She called him a coward. She said the money was nothing. She said she would rather starve. She said he had no right. Her fury went on for a long time and his replies did not. Then her voice changed and the fury was still there underneath but her grief had come up over the top of it and was louder, and after a while the talking stopped and there was the other sound, the sound of two people in a bed who had not much time left together. The fury did not go away. It was still in the apartment in the morning. It was in the apartment for the rest of Mom's life.

He had been the main course of a Sky-Council member's quarterly dinner. Mom had got the money. The body did not come back.

I had loved my father. I still loved my father. I did not let myself hold the other thing I felt about him, but the thing did not need my permission to be there. He had decided that the money was worth more than the man. He had decided that what he was to us was a resource and not a husband and not a father. He had signed the book on his crutches. He had told us at dinner. He had loved us, and loving us, he had left.

I finished the sandwich. I washed my hands at the bathroom tap. I strapped on the gauntlet and materialised the coat and the combat preset beneath it. The Powerstone's draw steadied as the armour came up around me. The helmet settled over my hair and the visor slit framed the room in its familiar narrow rectangle. The revolvers went on the belt. The swords at my hip and at the small of my back.

Greg was expecting me at Tully's by the third bell. I had a contract to hear about.
