# Voice Profile

Extracted from `source/manuscript.md`. The narrative voice the rewrite preserves while suppressing the bad habits catalogued in `polish-targets.md`.

## Narrative distance

First person, very close. The camera sits inside Marissa's skull at all times — past tense, retrospective with present-tense interjections, opinion and judgement and snark woven into the report of events without warning. The narrator is honest with the reader but is constantly editorialising: every observed person gets ranked, every choice gets second-guessed on the page, every body she touches gets graded. She narrates inner contradictions live (admiring a man and reminding herself she has come to kill him in the same paragraph).

Representative passage (ch.1, opening):

> I looked across the ballroom and cast a flirtatious glance at the tall man perusing the refreshments. He must have felt my eyes upon him for he suddenly stood up straight and scanned the crowd until he found me. I looked into his eyes for just a moment too long before I cast my eyes downward with a coy smile.

Why it works as distance: the camera is welded to Marissa's calculation — she is performing for Gereon and the reader simultaneously, and the prose lets us in on both the performance and the watching-the-performance. The voice never zooms out to summary; even macro events (the Fall, the war) arrive only through her remembered or inherited inner experience.

## Sentence rhythm

The default sentence is medium-to-long, declarative, comma-spliced or run on with "and" or "but". Short sentences come in clusters, almost always after a shock, a kill, or an emotional break ("I had killed Alissa.") and almost always to over-emphasise. There are many sentence fragments used as one-line paragraphs for punch-lines ("Mother.", "Time to fight. Time to die.", "I had to figure out a new story.").

Sentence openings skew SVO-and-conjunction: a great many sentences open with "I" or a coordinating conjunction ("And", "But", "So"). Participial openers exist but are not over-frequent. Where the source reaches for variety it does so through compound structures, not through rhetorical inversion.

What earns a fragment in this voice: realisation, reversal, deadpan punchline, a named emotion landing alone on its line. The narrator uses fragments self-consciously, often as the joke or the gut-punch of the paragraph.

Where long sentences land: in process and exposition — explaining a spell, describing magic-tech, walking through the math of a decision. Marissa thinks in long layered sentences when she is engineering, and in short ones when she is killing or grieving.

## Cadence

Paragraphs build by accumulation rather than rhetorical climb. Marissa starts an action, comments on it, reroutes into a related thought, returns to the action with another comment, and only resolves the paragraph when the practical thing has been done. Many paragraphs are very long blocks of mixed action, interiority, magic-system exposition, and inner monologue. The pacing-brake is almost always a one-line paragraph — either a fragment ("Mother?"), a single observation, or a quoted line of dialogue.

Where rhythm carries: action set pieces (the assault on the savage horde, the breach into the shield tower, the cliff-side fight with the two savages) where short and medium sentences alternate, dialogue interleaves, and exposition is suppressed.

Where it brakes for emphasis: emotional reveals, the death of a character, a mother-shade conversation. The cadence slows to one or two beats per line.

## Lexicon

Words and metaphor domains the author reaches for, kept as voice signature:

- **Magic-engineering vocabulary**: materialised magic, ley line, fount, shield, focusing tower, receiver, manufactorium, power core, spell stone, sigil/glyph/rune/diagram, scry/scrying, telekinesis, Blood Magic, meta-magic, Wild Magic. Used precisely, like terms of art. Keep.
- **Body / sensorium**: "groin", "nethers", "lap", "thigh", "boobs/breasts", "cleavage", "ass". Marissa is matter-of-fact and bawdy about bodies, hers and others'. Keep.
- **Military / mercenary register**: Guard, Captain, Sergeant, marines, breach, salient, redoubt, embrasure, broadside, magazine, bombardment cannon, anti-air. Keep — gives the voice its lived-in feel.
- **Personal idioms**: "fucked, and not the good kind", "shit show", "asshole", "bitch", "cunt", "motherfucker". Profanity is frequent and casual — Marissa swears in narration, dialogue, and interior monologue alike. Keep at lower density: the source uses it as a default punchline; the rewrite reserves it for real heat.
- **Cant from her parents**: "mother", "mom", "Bizhjerai", "Skylander", "Clansmen", "Now and forever", "Cursed be her name", "There are no innocent Skylanders". These are load-bearing — emotional anchors. Keep.
- **Self-deprecating diminutives**: "tiny", "petite", "short", "little lady", "slip of a girl". Marissa is short and is constantly noticed for it — by herself, by Zandor, by enemies. The voice teases her own stature relentlessly. Keep.
- **Engineering register for thought**: "ratio", "schematic", "blueprint", "iteration", "interface", "scaling", "prototype". When Marissa thinks, she thinks like an engineer. Keep — distinctive.

Words the source overuses (these go in `polish-targets.md`, not here): see polish-targets §1 for the live list. Tracked separately so the rewrite has a checklist.

## Punctuation habits

- **Em dash**: low frequency, almost never used as the AI pivot ("It wasn't X — it was Y"). The source uses em dash sparingly, usually as parenthetical insertion. Voice-correct: keep at low density, prefer commas or full stops.
- **Semicolon**: very rare. The source mostly uses period or "and" instead. Keep that.
- **Comma splice**: extremely common — many sentences run two or three independent clauses joined by commas. This is part of the breathlessness of the voice and should not be over-corrected, though the rewrite should break some of them into shorter sentences for variety.
- **Italics**: rare. Used for thought-aside emphasis only, never for telepathic dialogue. The source italicises a couple of times per chapter at most.
- **Ellipsis**: used for unfinished sentences in dialogue ("I…") and for trailing thought. Kept at moderate density.
- **Exclamation point**: high frequency in dialogue, especially Zandor's. Used to signal volume and personality of speaker. Keep, but trim — the source uses it on lines that don't need it.

## Recurring imagery

Motifs that travel across scenes and pay rent:

- **Sky / flight / falling**: Marissa flies in Angel armour. Valia falls. The Skylands fell, Utopeia fell, the Prowler falls. Looking up at flares, Skyships, mother-as-shade. The book's central physical motion.
- **Magic-as-pressure / magic-as-water**: power "billowing", "spilling", "torrent", "geyser", "drinking" magic, magic field "thinning", "draining". The cosmology consistently treats magic as a fluid the body and the world push and pull against.
- **Wind**: cuts, howls, whistles in ears during flight, rushes past while she falls. Often where a more abstract narrator would put "tension".
- **Breath**: held, drawn, gasped, hissed. The body-tell of choice in this voice — keep, but trim the "let out a breath she didn't know she was holding"-shaped clichés.
- **Mother's gem / dagger**: the talismanic object her thumb returns to throughout. The single most reliable physical anchor in the book; the rewrite should keep and lean into it.
- **The Bizhjerai Rune on her shoulder**: hidden, revealed, disguised, blazed in the final battle. Identity-as-marking.
- **Fire**: cauterising, ritual cremation, igniting tents, immolation in the cave. Frequent enough to read as motif rather than coincidence.
- **Crystal / crystalline**: every Skylander object is materialised-magic crystal — armour, gun stocks, bullets, Skyship hulls. Surfaces "tinkle", "shimmer", "glow". The world has a glassy palette that should carry into the rewrite.
- **Violet eyes / glow**: the mother-shade, the savages, the spell stones. The book's most-repeated visual signature.
- **Bodies as instrument**: Marissa weaponises her own body (flirting, seduction, costume, exposed cleavage as distraction). Part of the voice; the rewrite should keep it and not sanitise it.

## Voice anchors

**Anchor 1 — Ch.1, the kill (Marissa shoots Gereon).**

> In a single smooth movement I drew my revolver, cocked the hammer and aimed at the back of his head. His head had just started to turn toward the crystal tinkling of the hammer and I saw his bemused smile and confused frown when I squeezed the trigger. The weapon bucked in my hand, his hat flew off his head along with most of his brain. His body toppled bonelessly into the grass and lay still.

What works: action verbs do the carrying ("drew", "cocked", "aimed", "saw", "squeezed", "bucked", "flew", "toppled"). No adverbs. The fragmentary noticing of his expression ("bemused smile and confused frown") between trigger-pull and the wound landing is the close-third killer-eye the voice can do. The crystal-tinkling of the hammer is the voice's signature register: this world's machines sound like glass.

**Anchor 2 — Ch.13, hand on Zandor's head.**

> "Alright then, big guy. You guys carry on," I said and patted his curly locks as if he were a disgruntled boy. Damn was his hair soft and curly and I couldn't help marvelling at how his hairs felt as they slid across my palm. Warm and just a little bit damp from his sweat. I was standing so close that I could smell him. He smelled like hard work, earth and materialised magic. My fist clenched in his curls and I realised that I had hunched over him slightly to bring my face closer to his scent and that my boobs where practically in his face.

What works: the voice is intimately, unembarrassed-ly horny in a way that reads as character not author. The "Damn was his hair soft" inversion is colloquial Marissa, not literary affect. The catalogue of his smell uses the materials of this world ("materialised magic"). The realisation of her own boobs being in his face arrives at the same speed for her and the reader — observation, not anticipation. Sentence rhythm shifts from long (description) to short (gut realisation) without flagging it.

**Anchor 3 — Ch.20, the box.**

> I forced myself onto aching limbs and stood up only to promptly bash the top of my head on the ceiling. I collapsed with a curse and rubbed at my scalp. It was incredibly rare for me to ever strike my head against ceilings. If not even I could stand up straight in here then this cell was tiny indeed.

What works: the joke survives the bleakness. Marissa is in a cube she cannot stand up in and she pauses to be wry about being too short to ever hit her head on ceilings. The voice's resilience — the refusal to elevate even the worst moment into a register it doesn't naturally occupy — is the voice's signature mode. Keep.

**Anchor 4 — Ch.22, the cliff edge with Valia.**

> "I've known since I saw your Bizhjerai Rune. When I tucked you into bed after you exhausted yourself on the plains. I've known a long time who you are, and loved you anyway. You are a wonderful person Marissa. I don't hold your upbringing against you. You're with us now, that's all that matters."

What works: the rare moment when the voice lets another character speak at length without Marissa stepping on the line with commentary. Valia gets a clean run of sentences in her own register (shorter, declarative, soft). The strongest emotional beat in the book lands because Marissa shuts up. The rewrite must preserve that — the voice has the muscle to make room for other voices when it costs Marissa to listen.

**Anchor 5 — Ch.27, "I'm the last Skylander."**

> "I'm the last Skylander," I whispered.
>
> I didn't feel triumph. No relief. Just... Nothing. Empty.
>
> Everything I had worked for for more than half of my life I had now achieved. And I felt nothing. Not even a shred of satisfaction.
>
> Ten thousand Skylanders had died this day.

What works: the voice has been pointed at this single sentence for 130k words and when it arrives it does not over-decorate. The follow-up fragments do the emotional work — three short paragraphs of nothing, hollowed-out. The numerical specificity ("Ten thousand") is the voice at its most exact and most damning. Keep that exact-number habit; it's a load-bearing tic of the engineer-narrator.

## Voice anti-anchors

**Anti-anchor 1 — Ch.1, the page-long monologue dump (over Gereon's body).**

> I had set out on my journey almost a decade ago when I had relived my mothers life for the first time. That night when her spell beast had delivered the memory core on my pillow a year after mom had been killed in Utopeia and mother had killed herself to become a liche before flying away on my dragon brother to slay an evil monolith. I had seen my brother give his life to help mother take revenge for mom. I had felt his pain when the magic he drank consumed him from within. Two and a half million Skylanders had died that day, but thousands had survived the battle for Utopeia. Scattered into the Elweyr forest. And I had sworn on that day that I would find them all, one by one, and kill them. Every last one of them.

What's wrong: the worst case of action-stops-for-info-dump. The paragraph is most of a page of backstory parachuted in over the cooling corpse of the man she just killed. The rewrite must shorten the beat at the kill and let the backstory leak out across the next several chapters via memory, dialogue, and codex slices. The editorialising tic ("Scattered into the Elweyr forest. And I had sworn on that day…") is fine in small doses but the source piles paragraphs of it together.

**Anti-anchor 2 — Ch.12, magic-system over-explanation.**

> "Magic is a force," I started while remembering the explanation mother had given me as a child, later to be refined and expanded by Uncle Kenny. "Exactly how it works, we don't know. Maybe we did before the Apocalypse. But what we do know is that it somehow aggregates inside living creatures in what we call the Soul. This force, or power, is channelled through our bodies in response to our will."
>
> "I'm with you so far," Valia said with a cute smile that I returned.
>
> "Good, because here is where it gets weird. For simple things, like casting a light spell, pure will is enough and you just add magic and 'poof', instant light." […]
>
> "I'm not sure that I follow," Valia frowned.

What's wrong: classroom format inside a scene. The dialogue is asymmetric — Marissa lectures, Valia nods. The chapter ("Construction Mage") is the longest in the book (13.5k words) and stalls the narrative across two chapters of variations on this beat. The rewrite must redistribute the magic-system reveal across many scenes — leak it through what Marissa does, not what she says.

**Anti-anchor 3 — Ch.22, staccato action with reflexive body inventory.**

> "Reloading," Valia shouted and bowed her head to focus on her task.
>
> "Hold them," Wylah shouted before she stepped back to reload. Felaan had also expended his shots but instead of reloading he lowered my rifle in his left hand and he drew his revolver with his right to keep the pressure up. […] Valia where furiously reloading and Zandor where using his magic to hurl loose rocks over Zandor's head.

What's wrong: in action the voice over-reports limb-by-limb mechanics — exactly the "minutiae of action" failure mode the polish targets warn against. Every reload is described, every weapon switch announced. The reader does not need to hear the click of every bolt. The rewrite should pick the load-bearing motion (Zandor losing the haft of his halberd, Valia tripping over the egg) and let the rest of the choreography happen between the lines.

## Sample reframings

**Polish target — repetition.**

*Before (ch.12):*
> She asked a bunch of questions and I did my best to explain them all. I felt that I failed with many of them, my explanations being far too advanced for her sparse knowledge of magic. I was a poor teacher. As I spoke I tried to remember how Uncle Kenny had explained things to me, but I could not remember how he had worded it. I only remembered what he had taught me, not how.

*After:*
> She asked good questions. I answered them all badly. Whatever Kendall had done to teach me, I had not been paying attention to the how of it, only the what — and now Valia was watching me grope for the wording.

The shape "I felt that… I was a poor teacher" (named emotion + named judgement) is collapsed into the action.

**Polish target — staccato cadence.**

*Before (ch.6):*
> I stood there, naked and shivering, for a long moment. Then I giggled before I burst into gut-wrenching laughter.

*After:*
> I stood there, naked and shivering, until the laugh started somewhere in my belly and came up wrong, gut-deep and not at all funny.

Two clipped sentences collapsed into one longer sentence that lets the wrongness of the laughter land instead of just naming it.

**Polish target — action minutiae.**

*Before (ch.20):*
> I patted myself down. I still wore my Angel armour, though the main engines and the wings had been removed. When I reached over my shoulder I could feel the jagged ends of the mounting and I knew that they had been forcibly removed.

*After:*
> The wings were gone — the mounts on my shoulder blades came up jagged under my fingers when I checked.

Three sentences, one verb, no patting.

**Polish target — worldbuilding minutiae.**

*Before (ch.5):*
> The first prototype that Uncle Kenny and I had built had had the manoeuvrability jets mounted on the hands since Uncle Kenny wasn't comfortable with the skeletal arms, but I had argued that freeing the hands would make the armour much more useful. And it had.

*After:*
> [In the original scene, only the wings unfurling and her landing matters — the prototype history is moved to a later workshop scene with Valia, where it earns its place by foreshadowing the wing-fail in the cave.]

**Polish target — emotion-naming.**

*Before (ch.21):*
> The stab of panic I felt at the prospect of being returned to that dreadful box barely manage to pierce the thick blanket of darkness that enveloped my heart.

*After:*
> They had to drag me. My legs had decided. I went on my knees the first three steps and they hauled me the rest of the way to the box.

The named emotion ("panic") and the abstract metaphor ("thick blanket of darkness") are replaced by what the body does.

**Polish target — filler dialog tags.**

*Before (ch.4):*
> "Yes, please," I admitted bashfully.
> "Alright, please forgive me for being a little forward," Gereon muttered…

*After:*
> "Yes, please." I let it come out small.
> Gereon stepped close. "Forgive me for being a little forward," he said, almost meaning it.

Adverb-stacked tag replaced by an action beat; the second tag stays but is doing work (the "almost meaning it" is character).

**Polish target — mirrored / portentous scene closings.**

*Before (ch.27 lead-in):*
> Time to fight. Time to die.
>
> A large form burst out of the foliage and I raised my revolver to kill it.
>
> "Mary!" It cried…

*After:*
> A large form crashed out of the foliage. I had the revolver up before I'd thought about it. "Mary!" it called, and I knew the voice.

The "Time to fight. Time to die." portentous fragment is removed; the beat opens on the action and the recognition.
