# Voice Profile

Extracted from the three source books — `source/book-1-the-skyland-chronicles.md` (Kyra Zorantis, 51 chapters + prologue/epilogue), `source/book-2-the-last-skylander.md` (Marissa Zorantis, 28 chapters), and `source/book-3-epitome.md` (alternating Marissa and Barengar, 18 chapters + prologue + epilogue). The narrative voice the rewrite preserves while suppressing the bad habits catalogued in `polish-targets.md`.

The trilogy uses four voices in sequence. Each gets its own section below. There are habits that travel across all three narrators (long comma-spliced sentences, engineering precision, profanity, an arms-length self-irony) — those are author-level voice and the rewrite keeps them. There are habits that change between narrators (rhythm, register, lexicon, body-comedy frequency, distance from the action) — those are character-voice and the rewrite must hold each one separately.

Reviewers cite this file by section, e.g. `voice §Marissa.cadence` or `voice §Kyra.lexicon`.

## Kyra (source book 1)

### Arc-level register

Kyra's voice in book 1 is not a single setting. It tracks the trilogy's central arc — the woman who rediscovers love and beauty through Anjali, has both ripped from her at Utopeia, and finishes the book as the monster she was already becoming. The voice has four registers, and the rewrite must hold them distinct:

- **Pre-Anjali (book-1 opening, source ch.1–10)** — *brutal and jaded*. Kyra's humanity is at its lowest baseline. She is already partway down a bad road; the reader should feel it. The voice accounts; it does not pity. It does not defend or justify brutal acts — it walks past them. Tactical observation only; no internal pity for civilians; no defensive rationale for non-intervention. When Kyra walks past something horrible, she walks; she does not narrate her reasoning at length. When she kills, the kill is clinical, sometimes contemptuous, never apologetic. The "tradesperson assessing whether the day will end in a paycheck or a corpse" framing applies here, but with the harder edge — the tradesperson is also the kind of person who does not look down at what she just stepped over.
- **With Anjali (source ch.11–48)** — *softening but tactical*. The brutal-jaded baseline persists in her professional voice. In private with Anjali, the voice opens — tenderness in single-word sentences ("Wife."), a hint of self-amazement at being loved, body-comedy that is bawdy rather than callous. The tactical voice still rules the battlefield scenes; the softer voice belongs to the tent, the fire, the dark.
- **After Anjali (source ch.49 onward)** — *the monster, on-page*. The brutal-jaded baseline returns and exceeds itself. The Utopeia rampage, the Iolan execution, the dagger-suicide, the destruction of the Skylands. The voice now narrates atrocity in the same diagnostic register it used for the Stardust raid — but the scale has grown to genocide. The reader must feel that this is the same woman; that the journey from book-1 ch.1 to the Geyser is a straight line.
- **Spectral (books 2–3)** — the older, wearier voice documented in the §Kyra.spectral section below. The brutality has been earned. The regret has been worked through. The voice can afford gentleness now because there is nothing more it is going to do to anyone.

Kyra's humanity is *tied to love*. In pre-Anjali book 1 she has none of it (her mother is alive but distant; Greg is a colleague; Kendall is a friend at arms-length). She becomes a person again through Anjali; she stays a person, as a shade, through Marissa. The book's opening must establish what she is when love is absent — a woman who is already most of a monster, with a job to do and a city beneath her that she will eventually drown. The voice does not gesture at this; it lives there.

**Reviewer note**: a scene set in the pre-Anjali register must pass two negative tests. First, no internal pity for civilians (the cooking-floor women are observed, not mourned). Second, no defensive justification for brutal acts (Kyra walking past Anton's rampage does not narrate her reasoning at length; she walks). When either test fails, the register has drifted toward the spectral-Kyra of books 2–3, which is the wrong voice for the opening chapters.

### Narrative distance

First person, close, retrospective past tense. Kyra is older than Marissa (late twenties through early thirties across the book), already a working mercenary when the book opens, and the narration is wearier than her daughter's will be — less performative, more diagnostic. The camera is welded to her body and her judgements of other bodies (men's hands, other women's armour choices, her own breasts and how they have started to sag), but it is also welded to her professional eye: she ranks rooms by sight lines, gauges fights by what weapons the other side has, catalogues wounds.

She editorialises constantly but the editorialising is mostly tactical, not snarky. Where Marissa's interiority is the play-by-play of a con artist running a script, Kyra's is the play-by-play of a tradesperson assessing whether the day will end in a paycheck or a corpse.

Representative passage (ch.2, after she kills a child):

> Tears were streaming down my face and I turned from Greg in shame once I noticed. I started walking again. Greg was wise enough not to say anything until I regained my composure.

Why it works as distance: the noticing is delayed — she does not realise she is crying until she has already turned away. The voice tracks her body's responses one beat after they happen, which is the close-third for a character who does not narrate her own emotions to herself.

### Sentence rhythm

The default is medium-length, declarative, often comma-spliced. Kyra strings independent clauses together with commas more than Marissa does, and uses fewer fragments. When she opens a chapter she opens with action ("I pressed my back to the wall and glanced quickly around the corner"); when she opens a paragraph she opens with the next motion.

Fragments are rarer in Kyra than in Marissa. When they appear they are usually after a kill or a grief-beat ("Red was gone."). The book's longest sentence-cluster runs through her drug-raid set pieces — a single paragraph in ch.1 runs over a thousand words of fight choreography. The rewrite will need to break those up; the source author lets them swell.

Long sentences live in: post-battle wind-down and assessment, exposition about the Skylands, the city's economics, magic theory. Short sentences live in: grief (the death of the small girl, the death of Red, her own suicide), confrontation, and the cold-numb register she falls into after Anjali dies.

### Cadence

Paragraphs build by accumulation through to the late book; by ch.47–50 (the assault on Utopeia, Anjali's death, Kyra's transformation into a liche, the destruction of the Skylands) the cadence breaks down into staccato grief — many one-clause paragraphs of "She was gone. She was dead. She was gone." This is the voice's grief mode and the rewrite must keep it. What it must trim is the source's tendency to repeat the same staccato shape across multiple paragraphs without earning the second and third pass.

The voice's signature pacing-brake is not the punchline fragment (Marissa's habit) but the single observational sentence after a long passage of action: a noticing that reframes the scene. ("I had never tasted meat. Only the truly rich could.")

### Lexicon

- **Mercenary register**: "merc", "boss", "deal", "shift", "the cleanup", "post-battle fucking", "the trade", "the Skyway". Skylands-specific economic vocabulary that locates Kyra in her city before she ever leaves it.
- **Skylander hardware**: "Powerstone", "Soulstream", "gauntlet", "gorget", "corset" (the manifesting armour), "shield projector", "scry", "muzzle loader", "smoothbore", "repeating revolver". The same engineering vocabulary that Marissa will use later, but Kyra uses it as a user, not a designer. She knows what the tools do; Kendall is the one who makes them.
- **Body, casual and frank**: "boobs", "breasts", "cleavage", "ass", "thong", "groin", "nipple", "tit". Marissa inherited this. In Kyra it is matter-of-fact about the female body as a thing she has been paid for, fought with, and lives inside.
- **Clan vocabulary (acquired)**: "Clan", "Chief", "Huscarl", "Mark", "shaman", "Bizhjerai", "Outcast", "wyvern", "Drakonid", "Greater Dragon". Kyra learns these words across book 1 and the lexicon transition is itself voice — early chapters use Skylands vocabulary, mid-book she is still translating ("the spirits", "the elements"), late book she uses Clan terms without internal italics.
- **Profanity**: frequent, casual, with "fuck" and "shit" used as both adjective and intensifier. Less playful than Marissa's. Kyra swears for emphasis and from fatigue, not for performance.
- **Pet names (load-bearing)**: "Red" for Anjali (Kyra's invention, hated by everyone except Anjali). "Mom" for her Skylander mother (left behind, presumed dead in the Fall). The shift from "Mom" to no-mom is part of the book's arc.
- **Self-deprecation**: she is short by Skylander standards but taller than Anjali. She catches herself sagging, ageing, getting outpaced by younger mercs. Less reflexive than Marissa's height jokes.

### Punctuation habits

- **Em dash**: low. Kyra uses dashes for parenthetical insertions, almost never for the AI pivot.
- **Comma splice**: very high. Kyra strings independent clauses together with commas as a default — more frequently than Marissa does. The rewrite should keep some of this and break some, the same calibration as for Marissa.
- **Semicolon**: rare.
- **Italics**: rare. Used for thought-aside emphasis and for foreign words being introduced ("Sytrok" — the magical binding the Bizhjerai use on her). Italics are not used for telepathic dialogue.
- **Exclamation point**: moderate-low in narration, high in dialogue (especially Anjali's). Less than Marissa's tendency to exclaim in narration.

### Recurring imagery

- **Crystal and dark crystal**: the indigo/purple substrate of the Skylands. Walls, weapons, bullets, floor — everything is the same material. Kyra notes its colour, hardness, ability to absorb power. When she leaves the Skylands the absence of crystal is itself a visual disorientation.
- **Wood and earth and weather**: when Kyra arrives on the mainland these are uncanny. Wood smoke smells like burning meat. Soil under bare feet. Rain. The voice's job in mid-book is to render these as novel through the eyes of someone who has never touched a tree. **In pre-mainland chapters (ch.1–8), the absence is total** — Kyra does not reach for nature even as metaphor or comparison. The Skylands has materialised-magic crystal, fungus (the only living non-human matter on the city), and humans, in that order of abundance. Plants and animals are six-centuries-ago legend; if they appear in her thought at all, they appear with the wrong words (a horse is "a big dog" the first time she sees one — and she does not know what a dog is either, only that "big dog" is the phrase imported from a Skylands children's rhyme she half-remembers). No "clever as a fox", no "sharp-eyed as an eagle", no "rooted to the spot", no "branching path", no "fresh as a daisy", no "weeds", no "in full bloom". Even reflexive English idioms that bury a nature image — "dog-legged corridor", "ferreting out", "panning across" — are forbidden in pre-mainland register. Substitute with materials Kyra would actually know: crystal, fungus, brass, gauze, gut, ore, fume, glow-globe, the Skyway, the Geyser, the Bowels.

- **Lexical softening tracks the Anjali arc.** Once Kyra leaves the Skylands and starts living among the Bizhjerai (source-book-1 ch.9 onward), nature words enter her vocabulary one at a time — and **the narration itself softens as they do**. The brutal-industrial register and the no-nature lexicon are the same register; the soft register and the nature-word vocabulary arrive together. Each first-use of a nature word (her first "tree", her first "wolf", her first "rain") is a small step in the same arc as her first private moment with Anjali. Reviewers should clock the first-use moments and let them land as voice events, not as smooth uptake — the words come in unfamiliar, sometimes with the wrong gender or wrong shape, sometimes via Bizhjerai loanwords first. After Anjali dies (ch.49 onward), the brutal register returns but the nature vocabulary is already learned and stays — what changes is the affect. Trees can now appear in the prose; they appear cold.
- **Fire**: cremation pyres, magical fire as weapon, the burning of Anjali, Kyra setting the pyre alight with her own magic. The fire motif is shared with Marissa's book but Kyra's fire is mostly mourning and burial.
- **The dagger (Grasax's dagger)**: arrives as a captured artifact, becomes the vessel of Kyra's suicide-into-liche, becomes the gem Marissa carries through books 2 and 3. The single most reliable object in the trilogy and the load-bearing prop.
- **Wings and flight**: the Valkyrie armour, the dragons, soaring on Malkareth, falling to the ocean. The trilogy's central physical motion is established here, before Marissa is old enough to walk.
- **Red hair**: Anjali's. The murdered girl from chapter 1 also has red hair, which is the reason Kyra mistakes Anjali for her in the spear-meeting. The recurrence is load-bearing — a guilt-haunting that becomes a love-anchor. The rewrite must keep the chain.

### Voice anchors

**Kyra anchor 1 — Ch.1, after the raid (the encounter with Raynard).**

The post-battle scene with Raynard — a violent, exploitative quasi-consensual encounter on the floor of the murdered drug-cookers — is the book's introduction to Kyra's voice and the most challenging anchor. What works: the voice is honest about what she wants and how she hates herself for wanting it. The body inventory ("warmth splashed across my belly", "I rolled to my belly and pushed up to all fours") is voice-correct close-third — she is reporting her body's actions exactly as her brain registered them, including the moments where her body and her judgement are in opposite places. The voice does not flinch and it does not romanticise. The rewrite must preserve the honesty without inflating the leer; the source slips into pure description and the rewrite should keep the editorial.

**Kyra anchor 2 — Ch.2, the dead child.**

> Tears were streaming down my face and I turned from Greg in shame once I noticed. I started walking again. Greg was wise enough not to say anything until I regained my composure.

What works: the unselfconscious noticing of her own tears, the absence of a named emotion, the trust that Greg will understand without speech. This is Kyra's voice at its best — physical fact, social context, no interpretation. The rewrite's job is to find more of these and trim the source passages where Kyra over-explains why she is upset.

**Kyra anchor 3 — Ch.15, the first kill alongside Anjali.**

> "That was my first."
>
> I was puzzled. "First what?"
>
> "My first kill."
>
> "Really? That thrust was perfect, very practised."
>
> "It was so easy. I had thought it would be more difficult but he stood no chance."

What works: Kyra realises mid-conversation that Anjali has just killed a person for the first time, and the voice rotates from the professional ("very practised") to the careful (squeezing her shoulder, recognising the dazed look). The cross-character moment — Kyra the professional meets Anjali the novice — is the book's relationship in miniature. The economy is the model; the source elsewhere over-explains these moments.

**Kyra anchor 4 — Ch.49, Anjali's death.**

> "Anjali! Don't you dare let go. Fight it, damn you. Just let me think!" My thoughts were a jumble. I could see no way fix this. The dagger! […] Three words lingered in my mind. "Now and forever…"

What works: the voice in extremis goes short. Single-sentence paragraphs. The voice's lexical anchor ("Now and forever") is deployed for the first time in its full weight and the reader has been waiting for it for fifteen chapters. The fragmented thought-by-thought ("The dagger!", "Her energy was fading fast") tracks Kyra's panic exactly. The rewrite keeps the rhythm; it should trim some of the source's habit of having Kyra narrate her own grief immediately after ("She was gone. She was dead. Gone.") and let the absence do more of the work.

**Kyra anchor 5 — Ch.50, the suicide.**

> "Now and forever, Anjali. I love you." My whispered words faded, and I plunged the dagger home straight into my heart. There was no pain. My heart was already shattered to pieces near the shores of the Elwyer Forest, and now it was literally so.

What works: the joke survives the bleakness, just as in Marissa later. The "literally so" turn is gallows-dry and entirely voice-correct. The rewrite should resist polishing this into something more solemn — the joke is load-bearing.

**Kyra anchor 6 — Ch.50, the destruction of the Skylands.**

> A toddler girl, standing up from her crawl and taking her first stumbling step toward her father whose face lit up in wonder. He called out for his wife who where in the room nearby, relieving herself. […] I pulled harder.

What works: the voice's most damning move — the rolling magesense over the doomed Skylanders, naming small intimate moments of life that are about to end, and then "I pulled harder." after each. The juxtaposition is the trilogy's ethical claim and the source executes it with restraint. The rewrite should not over-decorate the small lives; let them be small and let "I pulled harder" do the work.

### Voice anti-anchors

**Kyra anti-anchor 1 — Ch.1, the long uninterrupted drug-raid paragraph.**

The opening fight runs over a thousand words in a single block with no paragraph breaks, describing every weapon switch, every bullet, every kick. It is impressive choreography and entirely unreadable. The rewrite must keep the violence and the voice but break the paragraph into rhythm units — when the lighting changes, when a new room is entered, when a new enemy is seen — so that the prose breathes.

**Kyra anti-anchor 2 — Ch.50, the post-Skylands monologue.**

Kyra narrates a full page of her dead-mother, dead-wife, dead-son retrospective while floating as an Archon over the falling Skylands. The voice has earned the moment but the source piles up every previous chapter as memory-list ("My first meeting with Red, how I had mistaken her for the girl I had murdered…"). The rewrite must compress and let only one or two memories land per beat, not the whole index.

## Marissa (source books 2 and 3)

### Narrative distance

First person, very close. The camera sits inside Marissa's skull at all times — past tense, retrospective with present-tense interjections, opinion and judgement and snark woven into the report of events without warning. The narrator is honest with the reader but is constantly editorialising: every observed person gets ranked, every choice gets second-guessed on the page, every body she touches gets graded. She narrates inner contradictions live (admiring a man and reminding herself she has come to kill him in the same paragraph).

Representative passage (book 2, ch.1, opening):

> I looked across the ballroom and cast a flirtatious glance at the tall man perusing the refreshments. He must have felt my eyes upon him for he suddenly stood up straight and scanned the crowd until he found me. I looked into his eyes for just a moment too long before I cast my eyes downward with a coy smile.

Why it works as distance: the camera is welded to Marissa's calculation — she is performing for Gereon and the reader simultaneously, and the prose lets us in on both the performance and the watching-the-performance.

**Shift in book 3.** Older Marissa (mid-50s, post-genocide, post-collegium-founder) keeps the close-third but the voice has cooled. The bawdy register is mostly gone (she still notices Barengar's body but the noticing is wistful, not appetitive). The engineering register has grown to the point that it now structures her interior monologue almost completely — when she thinks, she thinks in spell-frameworks, ratios, layer-counts. The voice is more pedagogical (she has been a teacher for decades and the habit of explaining is now reflex). The voice is also more isolated — book-2 Marissa narrates as someone with two lovers and a team; book-3 Marissa narrates as someone with one apprentice and one mother-shade and otherwise no one.

The rewrite should hold these as two distinct micro-modes within the same voice. When in doubt: if the scene is book-2-Marissa, the body and the appetite are on the page; if it is book-3-Marissa, the body has been mostly replaced with crystal prostheses and the appetite is residual, not active.

### Sentence rhythm

The default sentence is medium-to-long, declarative, comma-spliced or run on with "and" or "but". Short sentences come in clusters, almost always after a shock, a kill, or an emotional break ("I had killed Alissa.") and almost always to over-emphasise. There are many sentence fragments used as one-line paragraphs for punch-lines ("Mother.", "Time to fight. Time to die.", "I had to figure out a new story.").

Sentence openings skew SVO-and-conjunction: a great many sentences open with "I" or a coordinating conjunction ("And", "But", "So"). Participial openers exist but are not over-frequent. Where the source reaches for variety it does so through compound structures, not through rhetorical inversion.

What earns a fragment in this voice: realisation, reversal, deadpan punchline, a named emotion landing alone on its line. The narrator uses fragments self-consciously, often as the joke or the gut-punch of the paragraph.

Where long sentences land: in process and exposition — explaining a spell, describing magic-tech, walking through the math of a decision. Marissa thinks in long layered sentences when she is engineering, and in short ones when she is killing or grieving.

**Shift in book 3.** Sentence-length distribution flattens — fewer of the short punchline fragments, more of the long engineering layered sentences. The bawdy short joke is rarer. The voice has become more uniformly diagnostic. The rewrite should leave a few of the book-2 fragments in older Marissa as voice-echo (especially when Zandor reappears or when she remembers Valia or the Groundlands) so the reader hears that this is still the same narrator who once said "Mother."

### Cadence

Paragraphs build by accumulation rather than rhetorical climb. Marissa starts an action, comments on it, reroutes into a related thought, returns to the action with another comment, and only resolves the paragraph when the practical thing has been done. Many paragraphs are very long blocks of mixed action, interiority, magic-system exposition, and inner monologue. The pacing-brake is almost always a one-line paragraph — either a fragment, a single observation, or a quoted line of dialogue.

Where rhythm carries: action set pieces (the assault on the savage horde, the breach into the shield tower, the cliff-side fight with the two savages) where short and medium sentences alternate, dialogue interleaves, and exposition is suppressed.

Where it brakes for emphasis: emotional reveals, the death of a character, a mother-shade conversation. The cadence slows to one or two beats per line.

### Lexicon

- **Magic-engineering vocabulary**: materialised magic, ley line, fount, shield, focusing tower, receiver, manufactorium, power core, spell stone, sigil/glyph/rune/diagram, scry/scrying, telekinesis, Blood Magic, meta-magic, Wild Magic. Used precisely, like terms of art. Keep.
- **Body / sensorium**: "groin", "nethers", "lap", "thigh", "boobs/breasts", "cleavage", "ass". Marissa is matter-of-fact and bawdy about bodies, hers and others'. Keep. In book 3 the body inventory is mostly Barengar's body or Epitome's avatar; Marissa's own body is mostly catalogued as a list of prosthetics.
- **Military / mercenary register**: Guard, Captain, Sergeant, marines, breach, salient, redoubt, embrasure, broadside, magazine, bombardment cannon, anti-air. Keep — gives the voice its lived-in feel.
- **Personal idioms**: "fucked, and not the good kind", "shit show", "asshole", "bitch", "cunt", "motherfucker". Profanity is frequent and casual — Marissa swears in narration, dialogue, and interior monologue alike. Keep at lower density: the source uses it as a default punchline; the rewrite reserves it for real heat.
- **Cant from her parents**: "mother", "mom", "Bizhjerai", "Skylander", "Clansmen", "Now and forever", "Cursed be her name", "There are no innocent Skylanders". These are load-bearing — emotional anchors. Keep.
- **Self-deprecating diminutives**: "tiny", "petite", "short", "little lady", "slip of a girl". Marissa is short and is constantly noticed for it — by herself, by Zandor, by enemies. The voice teases her own stature relentlessly. Keep.
- **Engineering register for thought**: "ratio", "schematic", "blueprint", "iteration", "interface", "scaling", "prototype". When Marissa thinks, she thinks like an engineer. Keep — distinctive.
- **Book-3 additions**: "avatar", "memory core", "Reliquary of Souls", "Archangel", "intent control nexus", "meta-magic array", "pre-Apocalypse tome", and the half-borrowed mechanical vocabulary from the bunker computers — "electricity", "satellites", "geosynchronous", "GPS", "circuit". These read deliberately uncanny in Marissa's mouth because she does not always know what they mean. The rewrite should keep the uncanny.

### Punctuation habits

- **Em dash**: low frequency, almost never used as the AI pivot ("It wasn't X — it was Y"). The source uses em dash sparingly, usually as parenthetical insertion. Voice-correct: keep at low density, prefer commas or full stops.
- **Semicolon**: very rare. The source mostly uses period or "and" instead. Keep that.
- **Comma splice**: extremely common — many sentences run two or three independent clauses joined by commas. This is part of the breathlessness of the voice and should not be over-corrected, though the rewrite should break some of them into shorter sentences for variety.
- **Italics**: rare. Used for thought-aside emphasis only, never for telepathic dialogue. The source italicises a couple of times per chapter at most.
- **Ellipsis**: used for unfinished sentences in dialogue ("I…") and for trailing thought. Kept at moderate density.
- **Exclamation point**: high frequency in dialogue, especially Zandor's. Used to signal volume and personality of speaker. Keep, but trim — the source uses it on lines that don't need it.

### Recurring imagery

- **Sky / flight / falling**: Marissa flies in Angel armour. Valia falls. The Skylands fell, Utopeia fell, the Prowler falls. Looking up at flares, Skyships, mother-as-shade. The book's central physical motion.
- **Magic-as-pressure / magic-as-water**: power "billowing", "spilling", "torrent", "geyser", "drinking" magic, magic field "thinning", "draining". The cosmology consistently treats magic as a fluid the body and the world push and pull against.
- **Wind**: cuts, howls, whistles in ears during flight, rushes past while she falls. Often where a more abstract narrator would put "tension".
- **Breath**: held, drawn, gasped, hissed. The body-tell of choice in this voice — keep, but trim the "let out a breath she didn't know she was holding"-shaped clichés.
- **Mother's gem / dagger**: the talismanic object her thumb returns to throughout. The single most reliable physical anchor in the book; the rewrite should keep and lean into it.
- **The Bizhjerai Rune on her shoulder**: hidden, revealed, disguised, blazed in the final battle. Identity-as-marking.
- **Fire**: cauterising, ritual cremation, igniting tents, immolation in the cave. Frequent enough to read as motif rather than coincidence.
- **Crystal / crystalline**: every Skylander object is materialised-magic crystal — armour, gun stocks, bullets, Skyship hulls. Surfaces "tinkle", "shimmer", "glow". The world has a glassy palette that should carry into the rewrite. In book 3 the imagery becomes literal: Marissa's bones, fingers, eye, and skull-implants are all crystal.
- **Violet eyes / glow**: the mother-shade, the savages, the spell stones. The book's most-repeated visual signature.
- **Bodies as instrument**: Marissa weaponises her own body (flirting, seduction, costume, exposed cleavage as distraction). Part of the voice; the rewrite should keep it and not sanitise it. In book 3 this is residual — Barengar notes her cleavage and she does not stop him from looking, but she no longer uses it as a tool the way she did in book 2.

### Voice anchors

**Marissa anchor 1 — Book 2, ch.1, the kill (Marissa shoots Gereon).**

> In a single smooth movement I drew my revolver, cocked the hammer and aimed at the back of his head. His head had just started to turn toward the crystal tinkling of the hammer and I saw his bemused smile and confused frown when I squeezed the trigger. The weapon bucked in my hand, his hat flew off his head along with most of his brain. His body toppled bonelessly into the grass and lay still.

What works: action verbs do the carrying ("drew", "cocked", "aimed", "saw", "squeezed", "bucked", "flew", "toppled"). No adverbs. The fragmentary noticing of his expression ("bemused smile and confused frown") between trigger-pull and the wound landing is the close-third killer-eye the voice can do. The crystal-tinkling of the hammer is the voice's signature register: this world's machines sound like glass.

**Marissa anchor 2 — Book 2, ch.13, hand on Zandor's head.**

> "Alright then, big guy. You guys carry on," I said and patted his curly locks as if he were a disgruntled boy. Damn was his hair soft and curly and I couldn't help marvelling at how his hairs felt as they slid across my palm. Warm and just a little bit damp from his sweat. I was standing so close that I could smell him. He smelled like hard work, earth and materialised magic. My fist clenched in his curls and I realised that I had hunched over him slightly to bring my face closer to his scent and that my boobs where practically in his face.

What works: the voice is intimately, unembarrassed-ly horny in a way that reads as character not author. The "Damn was his hair soft" inversion is colloquial Marissa, not literary affect. The catalogue of his smell uses the materials of this world ("materialised magic"). The realisation of her own boobs being in his face arrives at the same speed for her and the reader — observation, not anticipation. Sentence rhythm shifts from long (description) to short (gut realisation) without flagging it.

**Marissa anchor 3 — Book 2, ch.20, the box.**

> I forced myself onto aching limbs and stood up only to promptly bash the top of my head on the ceiling. I collapsed with a curse and rubbed at my scalp. It was incredibly rare for me to ever strike my head against ceilings. If not even I could stand up straight in here then this cell was tiny indeed.

What works: the joke survives the bleakness. Marissa is in a cube she cannot stand up in and she pauses to be wry about being too short to ever hit her head on ceilings. The voice's resilience — the refusal to elevate even the worst moment into a register it doesn't naturally occupy — is the voice's signature mode. Keep.

**Marissa anchor 4 — Book 2, ch.22, the cliff edge with Valia.**

> "I've known since I saw your Bizhjerai Rune. When I tucked you into bed after you exhausted yourself on the plains. I've known a long time who you are, and loved you anyway. You are a wonderful person Marissa. I don't hold your upbringing against you. You're with us now, that's all that matters."

What works: the rare moment when the voice lets another character speak at length without Marissa stepping on the line with commentary. Valia gets a clean run of sentences in her own register (shorter, declarative, soft). The strongest emotional beat in the book lands because Marissa shuts up. The rewrite must preserve that — the voice has the muscle to make room for other voices when it costs Marissa to listen.

**Marissa anchor 5 — Book 2, ch.27, "I'm the last Skylander."**

> "I'm the last Skylander," I whispered.
>
> I didn't feel triumph. No relief. Just... Nothing. Empty.
>
> Everything I had worked for for more than half of my life I had now achieved. And I felt nothing. Not even a shred of satisfaction.
>
> Ten thousand Skylanders had died this day.

What works: the voice has been pointed at this single sentence for 130k words and when it arrives it does not over-decorate. The follow-up fragments do the emotional work — three short paragraphs of nothing, hollowed-out. The numerical specificity ("Ten thousand") is the voice at its most exact and most damning. Keep that exact-number habit; it's a load-bearing tic of the engineer-narrator.

**Marissa anchor 6 (book-3 voice) — Book 3, ch.9, Zandor returns.**

> "Zandor?" I wheezed through gritted teeth.
>
> He smiled, his burned flesh twisting that once so gentle grin into something from a nightmare.
>
> "So you do remember me, Marissa? I feared that you had cast me from your thoughts just as you cast me aside so long ago."
>
> It was him. It really was him. I had thought him dead at the destruction of the Groundlands. For thirty five years I had mourned his death.

What works: the book-2 Marissa survives inside the older woman. "It was him. It really was him." is the exact same short-cluster grief-shape that "I had killed Alissa." used in book 2. The thirty-five-year gap is given the load-bearing single line that the voice has earned. The rewrite must preserve this anchor — it is how the reader knows the same narrator is in the older body.

### Voice anti-anchors

**Marissa anti-anchor 1 — Book 2, ch.1, the page-long monologue dump (over Gereon's body).**

> I had set out on my journey almost a decade ago when I had relived my mothers life for the first time. That night when her spell beast had delivered the memory core on my pillow a year after mom had been killed in Utopeia and mother had killed herself to become a liche before flying away on my dragon brother to slay an evil monolith. […]

What's wrong: the worst case of action-stops-for-info-dump. The paragraph is most of a page of backstory parachuted in over the cooling corpse of the man she just killed. The rewrite must shorten the beat at the kill and let the backstory leak out across the next several chapters via memory, dialogue, and codex slices. The editorialising tic is fine in small doses but the source piles paragraphs of it together.

**Marissa anti-anchor 2 — Book 2, ch.12, magic-system over-explanation.**

> "Magic is a force," I started while remembering the explanation mother had given me as a child. […] "Exactly how it works, we don't know. […] aggregates inside living creatures in what we call the Soul. This force, or power, is channelled through our bodies in response to our will."
>
> "I'm with you so far," Valia said with a cute smile that I returned.

What's wrong: classroom format inside a scene. The dialogue is asymmetric — Marissa lectures, Valia nods. The chapter ("Construction Mage") is the longest in the book and stalls the narrative across two chapters of variations on this beat. The rewrite must redistribute the magic-system reveal across many scenes.

**Marissa anti-anchor 3 — Book 3, ch.3, the political-economics tutorial.**

> A form of governance called 'communism' had been attempted where the government owned everything and in theory supplied everything to everyone. That had ended in a death toll that was orders of magnitude greater than the Fall of the Skylands. […] Another system had been tried that they called 'capitalism' […] But it had eventually failed due to something the ancient people called 'corporatism' which I had not yet discovered the meaning of.

What's wrong: the older Marissa's interior monologue slides into Wikipedia-summary register about pre-Apocalypse political philosophy. The voice has become an essay narrator. The rewrite must compress: the political content can stay (it motivates Epitome) but it should arrive in fragments across scenes, in conversation with Barengar or Epitome, not as a lone Marissa monologue.

## Barengar (source book 3)

### Narrative distance

First person, close, present-to-the-action. Barengar is a man in his late twenties — student-mage at the Collegium, hunter-trained, axe-and-shield warrior, raised in the Clans. The voice is younger than older Marissa's and less self-ironic than younger Marissa's. He narrates without much front-of-mind editorial; the editorial mostly arrives one beat late, after he has done the thing. He is genuinely curious about Marissa, the world, and his own reactions, and the voice lets the reader see him being curious without his explaining the curiosity.

He has been Oathed to Marissa for most of the book, which means the narration is partially compromised — the reader can sometimes tell that Barengar's reactions are not entirely his own. The voice does not always know this. The ambiguity (which thoughts are his, which are the Oath's) is the book's interior mystery and is part of the voice.

Representative passage (ch.2):

> They were ugly bastards. Even the females was as tall as I was and while their hips were narrower than my own their shoulders was broader. Their skin was gray, their heads crowned in a thatch of bony spines that shuddered with a life of their own.

Why it works as distance: he is hunter-cataloguing. The taxonomy is voice — he registers the female-versus-male difference first (because he was trained to), then the structural features, then the unsettling detail. The grammatical slip ("the females was") is colloquial; the voice does not correct itself.

### Sentence rhythm

Barengar's sentences are shorter than either Marissa's or Kyra's. He defaults to SVO with frequent two-clause compound sentences. Fragments are common but they are not the punchline-fragments Marissa uses; Barengar's fragments are tactical noticing ("Clever.") and stage-direction beats ("My eyes wandered toward the door.").

Where the voice slows: when he is reasoning through something he does not yet understand (Marissa's crystal prosthetics, the Oath, what Epitome is). The paragraph builds at the speed of his realisation.

Where the voice quickens: in combat. His fight-scene prose is the cleanest in the trilogy — short verbs, no participial openers, no adverbs. The axe-and-shield fight against the savages in ch.2 is the book's clearest action choreography.

### Cadence

Barengar's paragraphs are shorter than the other two narrators'. He breaks on every mode-change. He also uses standalone one-sentence paragraphs (single observations: "Clever." / "Boring." / "Damn.") more frequently than Marissa, but they are observational rather than emotional.

The cadence shift in his late chapters (after the burning of the Oath tattoo, ch.18, then the killing of Marissa) lengthens — paragraphs sprawl, he reasons through guilt and consequence at length, and the voice loses some of its tactical economy. This is character: he has lost the structure the Oath provided and the prose is finding its own footing.

### Lexicon

- **Clan vocabulary (native)**: "Clansmen", "Chief", "buffalo", "the plains", "axe", "haft", "shield". His native register, used precisely.
- **Collegium / Mage vocabulary (learned, used with student-precision)**: "magesense", "Construction Magic", "Blood Magic", "meta-magic", "intent control nexus", "enchantment". The voice uses these correctly but Barengar always seems aware that he learned them recently.
- **Deoch draoidheil**: the Clan name for the savages — "magic-drinkers", literally. Barengar uses this term throughout. It is voice-marker. Marissa calls them "savages"; Barengar calls them "deoch draoidheil" except when paraphrasing Marissa. The two-name habit is identity.
- **Profanity**: present but lower density than either Marissa or Kyra. Mostly "fuck" and "shit" as intensifiers, rare as adjectives. Barengar swears in stress; he does not swear in commentary. This is a clear character marker — the voice should not drift toward Marissa-level profanity-as-punctuation.
- **Personal terms of address**: "mistress" for Marissa (the Oath term, used reverentially and then increasingly painfully). "Mother" for Kyra after she invites him to use it. "Jani" for his fiancée in Dun Traelak. The terms-of-address pattern is voice — Barengar marks rank and relationship in his vocabulary in a way Marissa does not.
- **Body / sensorium**: Barengar notices women's bodies (especially Marissa's, which is a continuing strain of the book) but the noticing is shy and apologetic — "I forced my eyes to not stare at her ass" — where Marissa would have just looked. This is a clear voice marker.

### Punctuation habits

- **Em dash**: rare. Barengar's voice uses dashes for sudden insertion only.
- **Comma splice**: moderate. Less than Marissa.
- **Italics**: very rare. Barengar uses italics for internal interjections only ("Clever."), not for emphasis.
- **Exclamation point**: low. Barengar is not an exclaimer. He notes things; he does not exclaim them.
- **Ellipsis**: used for trailing thought when he is uncertain or rocked.

### Recurring imagery

- **The axe**: hafted across his back, in his hand, lodged in a target, recalled telekinetically. The single physical anchor of his POV — the axe is to Barengar what the dagger is to Marissa. Always specific, always the same weapon.
- **Hands and shoulders**: his own and others'. He is a working-hands character — the prose returns to what his hands are doing more than what his face is doing.
- **Heat / cold**: the workshop's heat, the cold mountains where the deoch draoidheil live, the heating of the axe enchantment to cauterise. He registers temperature.
- **The Oath tattoo**: glowing on his shoulder, controlling his choices, eventually burned away. The most consequential prop in his POV.
- **Marissa's body, watched with restraint**: her hair, the cleavage, the crystal fingers, the burned ear, the crystal eye. Barengar's voice catalogues her over the book in a way that is slow-burn romantic without ever being declared. The pattern is voice and the rewrite must keep it.

### Voice anchors

**Barengar anchor 1 — Book 3, ch.2, the savage kill.**

> "Come one then, bitch!" I taunted her even though I knew that she would not understand me.

What works: the voice is full of physical confidence and a teenager's bravado without being a teenager. The taunt is part nervous-energy, part professional. The bracket ("even though I knew that she would not understand me") is voice-correct — he registers the dissonance one beat after the act and keeps moving. The rewrite must keep this exact register; it is the cleanest example of Barengar's tone.

**Barengar anchor 2 — Book 3, ch.2, the first meeting with Marissa.**

> Whatever I had expected, it was not this. Her face was youthful and I would not have believed that she was much older than I even though I knew that she was in her mid fifties. One of her almond eyes, the right one, was a lovely brown colour that almost perfectly matched the colour of her hair while the other was entirely made out of blue crystal that flickered with an inner light.

What works: the voice catalogues Marissa with hunter's eye, then notices the crystal eye and slows to register what it implies. The rhythm of "noticing → understanding → re-noticing" is Barengar's voice in miniature. The rewrite should preserve this — the moment Barengar sees Marissa for the first time is the book's romantic-hook and the source executes it without dialogue and without commentary.

**Barengar anchor 3 — Book 3, ch.6, the conversation with Kyra-shade.**

> "She is troubled by her past and obsessed with making amends by turning the future into some sort of utopia."
>
> "A sentiment shared," Kyra murmured. "I too held such dreams once, before the cruel reality of the world struck such thoughts from my mind."
>
> "What happened?" I asked, belatedly remembering her story.

What works: the "belatedly remembering" tag is the voice's signature noticing-late move. Barengar is direct and economical with Kyra in a way Marissa is not, and the dialogue rotates Kyra into a different register than her own narration showed her. The rewrite should keep moments where Barengar's voice forces other characters into clarity — it is one of the load-bearing functions of his POV.

**Barengar anchor 4 — Book 3, ch.18, burning the Oath off.**

> I strained and summoned fire into my palm. Scorching heat sent spikes of pain up my neck and into my brain and my ears filled with the sound of sizzling meat in a hot pan. The skin and flesh beneath my palm bubbled and hissed and scalded my palm. But as the tattoo was destroyed I felt my mind become my own again.

What works: physical fact, no metaphor, the voice does not pretend the pain means anything other than pain. The release is described in the negative ("my mind become my own again") because the voice does not have a positive vocabulary for autonomy — he has been Oathed since his apprenticeship began and has nothing to compare freedom to. The rewrite must preserve this.

**Barengar anchor 5 — Book 3, ch.18, killing Marissa.**

> Snarling and gasping for air in a spreading pool of boiling blood the Last Skylander died.
>
> My mistress.
>
> My teacher.
>
> The best friend that I'd had my whole life.
>
> Dead by my hand.

What works: the fragment-list cadence Marissa uses for grief is borrowed here, but the items are vocational ("mistress", "teacher") rather than relational, which is exactly Barengar's voice — he ranks people by what they were to him in a guild structure. The progression from title to title to "best friend" earns the final tag. The rewrite must keep this — it is the book's strongest single passage.

### Voice anti-anchors

**Barengar anti-anchor 1 — Book 3, ch.4, the long political-summary thoughts about the Council.**

Barengar in the Council chambers has a tendency to narrate his political theorising in the same chapter where the political action is happening. The voice should not be doing analysis at the same pace as the dialogue; the rewrite must compress.

**Barengar anti-anchor 2 — Book 3, ch.10–16, the body-noticing of Marissa as recurring reflex.**

Barengar catches himself looking at Marissa's body multiple times a chapter across the middle of the book. Once is character, twice is voice; the source overruns. The rewrite must cap at one body-noticing per scene and let the others land through what Barengar is *doing* (standing closer, lingering at her workshop door) rather than what he is reporting himself to be noticing.

## The Epitome (source book 3, epilogue only)

The closing frame is the briefest of the four narrators — a single ten-page chapter at the end of book 3. The Epitome of Morality narrates from orbit, having absorbed Marissa's soul-fragment after Marissa's death. The voice is uncanny by design: it inherits memories and emotions from both Kyra and Marissa but it is not either of them. It is patient where they were urgent. It is moral-philosophical where they were practical. It is plural-implicit — every "I" refers to a composite of three (Epitome + Kyra + Marissa) and the voice never quite resolves which one is speaking on any given line.

The voice is short. There is not a lot of it. The rewrite should:

- Keep the first-person ("I am the Epitome of Morality") but resist filling out a fuller voice — the brevity and the haunted ambiguity are the point.
- Avoid making the Epitome a god-narrator. She has a body, in orbit. She has resource limits. She is afraid of Barengar.
- Keep the ledger-clean cadence of her interior monologue — sentences are short, evaluative, almost biblical: "I would work tirelessly on these two goals. To create Utopia in life. And Utopia in death." The voice's signature is this declarative one-sentence-per-paragraph mode.

**The Epitome anchor — Ch.19, the closing lines.**

> I realized that I could also create eternal torment in the afterlife if I so chose.
>
> Some people would undoubtedly deserve it, and the knowledge that such was the fate of those who were wicked would dissuade them from being evil in life.
>
> I know how to separate the good from the evil.
>
> I know who would deserve eternal torment and who would deserve perpetual bliss.
>
> I am the Epitome of Morality.

What works: the trilogy ends on a voice that has been pieced together from two flawed humans and one machine, and the voice is calmly contemplating eternal torment as a policy lever. The horror lands because the cadence is calm. The rewrite must not editorialise this — let the voice do the work, and let the reader recognise what has been built.

## Cross-narrator notes

Things that hold across all three narrators (author-level voice, keep):

- The long comma-spliced sentence as default.
- Engineering precision in the description of magic and machinery.
- Casual profanity (with character-varied frequency — Marissa highest, Kyra middle, Barengar lowest, Epitome zero).
- "Now and forever" as the inherited verbal tic of the Zorantis line.
- The dagger / liche-gem as the load-bearing physical anchor (Kyra forges it, Marissa carries it, Epitome eats it).
- Action verbs doing the carrying. Adverbs are rare in all three voices.
- Body-as-instrument honesty — Kyra and Marissa both narrate their own bodies in unsanitised, unromantic detail; Barengar narrates other bodies with a hunter's eye.

Things that change between narrators (character-voice, hold separate):

- Fragment frequency: Marissa highest, Kyra moderate, Barengar low (and observational rather than emotional), Epitome only as declarative ledger.
- Self-deprecation: Marissa makes a running joke of her height; Kyra catches herself ageing; Barengar is shy about being attracted to women; Epitome does not self-deprecate.
- Editorialising distance: Marissa narrates while running the script; Kyra narrates assessing the room; Barengar narrates one beat behind the action; Epitome narrates from outside time.
- Magic-system register: Kyra uses it as a user; Marissa uses it as a designer; Barengar uses it as a learner; Epitome is a magic-system unto herself.

If any rewrite scene has more than one of these POVs in play (only book 3 alternates), the writer's brief must specify which voice is on the page and the reviewer must enforce. Cross-POV bleed (Marissa narrating in Barengar's chapter, or Kyra-shade narrating outside book 1) is the single most common reviewer-flag risk for the trilogy.
