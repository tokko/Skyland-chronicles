# Polish Targets

Bad habits to suppress in the rewrite. Extracted from the source manuscript. These are inherited from the source author's voice; the rewrite keeps the voice but smooths these out.

## 1. Word and phrase repetition

The source repeats specific words or phrases across nearby sentences and paragraphs. The rewrite varies them. Within a single page, no signature word should repeat more than its meaning earns.

**Watch for**:

- **"suddenly" / "sudden"** — extreme over-use. Often the sentence reads better without it; when an event genuinely is sudden, the verb should do the carrying. Cap at one or two per scene.
- **"of course"** — concessive throat-clearing, used by narrator and several characters interchangeably so it stops being characterising. Cut on sight unless inside a specific speaker's voice.
- **"indeed"** — confidence-buffer, often paired with "of course". Cut.
- **"perhaps"** — Marissa hedges aloud. Keep when she is genuinely uncertain; cut when it is filler.
- **"a bit" / "a little" / "a little bit"** — frequent hedge. Trim to one form per scene at most.
- **"sort of" / "kind of"** — same hedge-class as "a bit". Use only when the speaker is actually approximating.
- **"I sighed" / "I shrugged" / "I nodded"** — interchangeable default beats. Cap each at once per scene; replace with a beat that does more work (what the hands are doing, what the eyes are doing, where the body is in the room).
- **"materialised magic" / "Construction Mage" / "spellstone" / "powerstone"** — load-bearing nouns over-stacked in any magic-system scene. After the first naming in a scene, vary: "the spell", "the stone", "the work", or just the pronoun. Same applies to "Skylander" and "Bizhjerai" when stacked in dialogue.
- **"the big man" / "the big guy" / "the large man"** — default Zandor epithets. Use his name; vary the epithet only when it does real work.
- **"mother" / "mom"** — load-bearing terms but the source uses both within the same exchange to the point of blur. The rewrite must distinguish them clearly: "mom" is the Bizhjerai birth-by-adoption mother (Anjali / Red Terror); "mother" is the Skylander mother (Kyra / Bás Marbhadair / the shade in the dagger). When ambiguity threatens, use the name or "Red".
- **"truly" / "actually" / "really"** — Marissa's stress-emphasisers, used to insist on her own claims. Trim to once per scene.
- **"with a / wearing a / sporting a [adjective] [noun]"** — frequent character-tagging shape ("with a worried frown", "wearing a sly grin"). Vary or cut.
- **"my mothers" (plural possessive)** — load-bearing term of art (both Kyra and Anjali). Keep; do not "correct" to singular. But watch for accidental ambiguity when only one of them is in the scene.
- **"smirk / smirked / smirking"** — high-frequency facial tag. Use sparingly; prefer specific action.
- **"chuckled"** — every male character chuckles. Pick speaker-distinct laugh-words or drop the tag.
- **"to my surprise" / "to my great surprise" / "I was surprised"** — used as a transition rather than an observation. Cut unless the surprise is itself the point.
- **"Skylander way" / "the way of the Skylanders"** — thematic phrase. Keep, but the source reaches for it every time any character disapproves of anything. Vary by reference; let the act speak.
- **"Anyway" / "Anyways"** — narrator throat-clearing between thoughts. Cut.
- **"the fuck"** as filler intensifier ("what the fuck", "where the fuck", "passed the fuck out"). Frequent in source. Keep at much lower density: reserve for genuine alarm, not as comic punctuation.
- **"fucked, and not the good kind"** — recurring punchline-template. Used twice in the source it is a voice tic; used four or more times it is a self-parody. Cap at one occurrence per book.

**Fix**: substitute, restructure, or cut. If the word must repeat for resonance, make the repetition load-bearing — not accidental.

## 2. Staccato cadence

Strings of short clipped sentences without variation. Reads like a machine gun. Loses the modulation that lets the reader feel weight.

**Fix**: vary sentence length deliberately. A staccato burst is a tool, not a default. Earn it with a long sentence before, a long sentence after.

**Before (typical source)**:
> He walked. The wind cut. He kept going. His hand hurt. He didn't look down.

**After**:
> He walked, and the wind cut him crosswise as he went. His hand hurt — he didn't look at it.

## 3. Action minutiae

Step-by-step blocking that records every motion. The reader doesn't need the choreography; they need the consequential motion.

**Before**:
> He lifted his left hand, then his right, then placed both on the table, palms down. He took a breath. He looked up. He met her eyes.

**After**:
> He set his hands on the table and met her eyes.

**Fix**: keep only the motion that carries meaning. Drop the bridging mechanics.

## 4. Worldbuilding minutiae

Loving inventories of detail that aren't paying their rent. A copper kettle with three rivets and a brass handle, when the scene is about whether someone is about to confess.

**Fix**: detail earns its place by doing one of three things — establishing mood, foreshadowing, or revealing character. If a detail does none of those, cut it.

## 5. Emotion-naming

Telling us a character "felt afraid", "was angry", "experienced doubt". Reads flat and reads AI-adjacent.

**Fix**: show what the body does, what the eye lands on, what the thought-shape becomes. Trust the reader to name it.

## 6. Filler dialog tags

"He said. She said. He said. She said." cluster, or worse: "he chuckled / muttered / quipped / exclaimed" carousel.

**Fix**: `said` is invisible and that's a feature. Use action beats instead of tags when you need to break up exchange. When characters have distinct voices, tags become optional.

## 7. Mirrored scene closings

Scenes that end by restating the image they opened with. Reads symmetrical in the bad way — mechanical, AI-trained.

**Fix**: let scenes end on the new thing, not the old.

## 8. Magic-system lecture mode

The source's longest chapter ("Construction Mage", 13.5k words) is largely Marissa explaining magic to Valia in a Socratic exchange ("I'm with you so far", "Now it gets weird"). The reveals are interesting but the format collapses scene into classroom: one character lectures, the other nods, the world holds still.

**Fix**: redistribute the magic-system reveal across many scenes. Show Marissa doing the thing before naming it. When a term must be named, let it cost something — a misfire, a question Marissa cannot answer, a glance Valia returns. Never two beats of lecture in a row without an action turning the page.

## 9. Mother-shade as crisis-only therapist

Every appearance of the dead-mother shade in the source serves the same scene-shape: Marissa is in despair, the shade arrives, the shade comforts and dispenses moral clarity, the shade fades. The format flattens into a recurring confessional booth. The shade also tells the reader directly how Marissa should feel about her own actions.

**Fix**: vary the function. Sometimes the shade should be wrong, or sharp-tongued, or unable to comfort. Sometimes she should ask Marissa something the reader does not yet know. Cut at least one of the source's shade appearances entirely; promote at least one into a scene where the shade does not solve a problem.

## 10. Calling-attention-to-being-short

Marissa's height is real character and a source of voice. The source over-fishes it: nearly every chapter contains at least one beat where she notes she is short, someone else notes she is short, or she compares herself to a taller person. The third "she had to tilt her head back" beat per chapter is the AI-density problem.

**Fix**: cap at one height-tell per scene. Where the source uses height as setup for a joke, keep it; where it is reflexive ("I had to crane my neck to look up at him"), cut or vary.

## 11. Sexual / body-comedy as default break

The source uses "the cheek slid down my thigh", "fucked, and not the good kind", boob-window-of-the-blouse, etc. as comic relief at high frequency. The voice is bawdy; that is a feature. The over-frequency is the bug.

**Fix**: keep the bawdiness but space it out. One body-comedy moment per scene at most; lean into the awkward, the failed flirt, the bad timing, rather than the pure leer.

## 12. Long block paragraphs

Many source paragraphs run 300–600 words combining action, exposition, interiority, and speech-act. The voice is fine with long paragraphs but the source default goes too long, especially in the magic chapters. The visual density alone reads exhausting.

**Fix**: when a paragraph crosses ~200 words, split on the natural mode-change (action → thought, thought → dialogue, decision → execution). The voice keeps its layering but the reader gets paragraph breaks as breathing room.

## 13. Portentous one-line closers

"Time to fight. Time to die." / "Time to go home." / "Time to get to work." / "I had work to do that was not research." — closing sentences that announce mood instead of inhabiting it. The source uses one per chapter at minimum.

**Fix**: cut all "Time to X" closers on first pass; if the chapter truly needs the gear-shift, find an image or a specific action rather than a register-shift.

## 14. Marissa says "I lied"

The source repeatedly attaches an explicit "(I lied.)" / "I lied to her" / "It was a lie" to Marissa's deceptions. This is the close-third version of telling-the-reader-the-subtext. The reader already knows she is lying; the tag flattens the irony.

**Fix**: cut the explicit "I lied" tag except where Marissa is registering it to herself for the first time. Let the lie do its work; let the reader catch it without the footnote.
